ENVIRONMENTAL HAZARDS 
Introduction 
As we progress through rapid industrialization, urbanization, and technological 
advancement, the interaction between human activities and the natural environment has 
intensified.  
While development has  improved livin g standards, it has also led to significant 
environmental challenges that threaten ecosystems, human health, and global 
sustainability. 
Environmental hazards are not a new phenomena.  
However, their frequency, intensity, and impact have increased dramatically over the past 
centuries. 
From air pollution in urban cities , to climate-induced disasters such as floods, droughts, 
and wildfires, environmental hazards affect all living beings and ecosystems across the 
planet. 
Understanding environmental hazards is essential for several reasons.  
i. It helps us identify the risks associated with natural and human-induced activities. 
ii. It allows policymakers, scientists, and communities to develop strategies to mitigate 
and manage these risks.  
iii. It empowers individuals to adopt sustainable practices that reduce environmental 
degradation. 
Environmental hazards are often interconnected. For example, deforestation can lead to 
soil erosion, which in turn increases the risk of floods. Simila rly, industrial emissions 
contribute to air pollution, which can cause respiratory diseases and contribute to climate 
change. 
Another important concept is risk, which refers to the probability of a hazard causing harm. 
Risk depends on both the magnitude of  the hazard  and the vulnerability of the 
population or ecosystem exposed to it.

1. Definition & Classification of Environmental Hazards 
1.1 Definition of Environmental Hazards  
Environmental hazards refer to natural or human-made events or conditions that pose 
a threat to the environment, human health, or ecological systems . These hazards can 
disrupt ecosystems, damage infrastructure, and lead to loss of life and property. 
i.e. Environmental hazards are physical, chemical, biological, or social conditions or events 
in the environment that have the potential to harm human health, cause injury, damage 
property, or degrade natural ecosystems. 
Environmental hazards can be broadly categorized into two groups: 
1. Natural hazards  
2. Anthropogenic (human-induced) hazards  
Natural hazards occur due to natural processes of the Earth, such as earthquakes, volcanic 
eruptions, floods, and hurricanes. While these events are inevitable, their impact can be 
amplified by human activities. 
Anthropogenic hazards, on the other  hand, arise from human actions such as industrial 
pollution, deforestation, improper waste disposal, and excessive use of natural resources. 
A key feature of environmental hazards is their potential to cause harm. This harm can 
manifest in various forms, including: 
 Physical damage to ecosystems  
 Health problems in humans and animals  
 Economic losses  
 Long-term environmental degradation  
 Environmental hazards can be classified into several types based on their origin and 
nature.

1.3 Physical Hazards 
Physical hazards are natural processes or events that can cause physical damage. While 
often classified as natural disasters, climate change is increasing their frequency and 
intensity. 
These include: 
a) Geological: Earthquakes, tsunamis, volcanic eruptions, landslides 
b) Meteorological: Hurricanes/cyclones, tornadoes, blizzards, extreme heat/cold 
c) Hydrological: Floods, storm surges, droughts 
d) Radiation: UV radiation (skin cancer), ionising radiation (nuclear accidents), radon gas 
e) Noise: Occupational hearing loss, cardiovascular disease from chronic noise exposure

These hazards are often unpredictable and can cause widespread destruction. For example, 
floods can submerge large areas, destroying crops, homes, and infrastructure. 
2. Chemical Hazards 
2.1 Overview 
Chemical hazards arise from exposure to harmful chemicals in the environment. These 
include: 
i. Air pollutants such as carbon monoxide, sulfur dioxide, and nitrogen oxides  
ii. Water contaminants like heavy metals (lead, mercury, arsenic)  
iii. Soil contamination from pesticides and industrial waste  
2.2 Sources 
i. Industrial: Factory effluents, mining runoff, manufacturing waste containing solvents, 
acids, heavy metals 
ii. Agricultural: Pesticides (organochlorines, organophosp hates), herbicides, synthetic 
fertilizers 
iii. Consumer products: Plastics (BPA), personal care products (phthalates, parabens), 
cosmetics 
iv. Food industry:  Synthetic food dyes (tartrazine, sunset yellow), preservatives, food 
contact materials 
v. Pharmaceuticals: Active drug compounds in wastewater: 'emerging contaminants' 
vi. Fossil fuels: PAHs, benzene, toluene, lead from combustion 
 
2.3 Routes of Human Exposure 
Chemical hazards can enter the human body through inhalation, ingestion, or skin contact, 
leading to serious health issues such as cancer, neurological disorders, and organ damage.

2.4 Mechanisms of Chemical Harm 
i. Endocrine disruption: Chemicals mimic or block hormones → reproductive, 
developmental effects 
ii. Carcinogenesis: DNA damage, adduct formation, epigenetic changes → cancer 
iii. Neurotoxicity: Impair neural development and function — lead, methylmercury, 
organophosphates 
iv. Organ toxicity: Direct damage to liver (hepatotoxins), kidneys (nephrotoxins), lungs 
v. Reproductive toxicity: Impair gamete production, fertility, foetal development 
vi. Immunotoxicity: Suppress or dysregulate immune system (e.g., dioxins, PCBs) 
 
2.5 Chemical Hazard Case Study 1: BPA (Bisphenol A) 
BPA (Bisphenol A) is a synthetic oestrogen and endocrine-disrupting chemical (EDC) used 
in polycarbonate plastics and epoxy resin since the 1960s. Global production exceeds 8 
million tonnes per year. It is one of the most studied and controversial chemicals in modern 
toxicology. 
 
 

=== page 6 ===
6 
 
 
2.5.1 Where We Are Exposed 
i. Polycarbonate water bottles (plastic #7) — BPA leaches when heated 
ii. Food and beverage can linings — epoxy resin coatings containing BPA 
iii. Thermal paper (ATM receipts, boarding passes) — BPA on surface, absorbed through 
skin 
iv. Baby feeding bottles — now banned in EU, US, Canada, and India 
v. Dental sealants, CDs, DVDs, medical devices, food storage containers 
 
2.5.2 Health Effects 
i. Oestrogenic activity:  BPA binds oestrogen receptors (ERα, ERβ) at very low 
concentrations (picomolar range) 
ii. Hormonal disruption: Causes early puberty in girls, reduced sperm count and quality 
in males 
iii. Cancer: Linked to increased risk of breast cancer (oestrogen -sensitive) and prostate 
cancer 
iv. Metabolic disease: Associated with obesity, insulin resistance, and Type 2 diabetes 
v. Cardiovascular: Associated with hypertension and coronary artery disease 
vi. Neurodevelopmental: Affects brain development — anxiety, aggression, impaired 
memory in animal models 
vii. Reproductive: Linked to polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) and endometriosis 
 
Key concern: The 'low dose' hypothesis: BPA shows effects at concentrations fa r below 
what was traditionally considered 'safe'. Even nanomolar concentrations can activate 
hormonal pathways in sensitive developmental windows.

2.7 Chemical Hazards Case Study 3: Phthalates 
Phthalates (phthalic acid esters) are plasticisers - chemicals added to PVC (polyvinyl 
chloride) to make it soft and flexible. They constitute 25–50% of flexible PVC by weight. 
Global production exceeds 8 million tonnes per year. As they  are not chemically bonded 
to the plastic, they leach out continuously. 
2.7.1 Sources of Exposure 
i. Flexible PVC products: Vinyl flooring, shower curtains, garden hoses, artificial leather 
ii. Medical devices: IV bags, blood transfusion tubing, catheters (DEHP — most studied 
phthalate) 
iii. Personal care products: Perfumes, hairsprays, nail polishes (DBP — dibutyl phthalate) 
iv. Children's toys: Soft vinyl toys, bath toys, teething rings 
v. Food packaging: Phthalate contamination from plastic wraps, food processing 
equipment 
vi. Building materials: Wall coverings, flooring — inhaled as dust and absorbed dermally 
 
2.7.2 Health Effects

oestrogenic effects of BPA

4.2 Biological Hazard Case Study 1: Malaria 
Malaria is a life-threatening parasitic disease caused by Plasmodium protozoa, transmitted 
through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. WHO 2022: 249 million cases; 
608,000 deaths: 76% in children under 5 in sub-Saharan Africa. 
4.2.1 Causative Agent 
i. Plasmodium falciparum: Most lethal species, causes cerebral malaria, severe anaemia, 
multi-organ failure. Responsible for >90% of malaria deaths. 

=== page 10 ===
10 
 
ii. Plasmodium vivax:  Most widespread; can relapse years later due to dormant liver 
stages (hypnozoites). Common in South Asia and Latin America. 
iii. Plasmodium malariae: Chronic infection; rare but causes kidney damage 
iv. Plasmodium ovale: West Africa; similar to P. vivax with relapses 
v. Plasmodium knowlesi: Zoonotic, from macaque monkeys in Southeast Asia; can be 
fatal 
4.2.2 Life Cycle & Transmission 
The malaria life cycle alternates between the human host and the female Anopheles 
mosquito vector: 
Phase 1: Exo-erythrocytic (liver stage): Sporozoites injected by mosquito → travel to liver 
→ infect hepatocytes → multiply asexually → release merozoites into bloodstream (7–10 
days) 
Phase 2: Erythrocytic (blood stage): Merozoites infect red blood cells (RBCs) → ring form 
→ trophozoite → schizont → rupture RBCs → repeat cycle every 48 –72 hours (fever 
spikes coincide with this) 
Phase 3 : Gametocytes: Some merozoites develop into gametocytes → ingested by 
mosquito → fertilization in mosquito gut → oocyte → sporozoites → salivary glands → 
transmitted to next human host 
 
4.2.3 Clinical Features 
i. Classic fever: High temperature (39–41°C) with chills and rigors, occurring in 48 -hr 
cycles 
ii. Anaemia: Destruction of RBCs (haemolytic anaemia) — weakness, pallor, fatigue 
iii. Splenomegaly: Enlarged spleen from increased RBC destruction and immune 
activation 
iv. Severe malaria (P. falciparum):  Cerebral malaria ( coma, seizures), severe anaemia 
(Hb <7 g/dL), acute respiratory distress, hypoglycaemia, multi -organ failure — fatal 
within 24 hours if untreated 
v. Pregnancy: Placental malaria — maternal death, low birth weight, stillbirth

4.3 Biological Hazard Case Study 2: COVID-19 
COVID-19 (Coronavirus Disease 2019) is caused by SARS -CoV-2, a novel 
betacoronavirus first identified in Wuhan, China in December 2019. The WHO declared it 
a pandemic on 11 March 2020. As of 2024: 700+ million confirmed cases; ~7 million 
official deaths (true death toll estimated at 15–20 million by excess mortality analysis). 
4.3.1 The Pathogen 
i. SARS-CoV-2: Single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus (genome ~30 kb). Enveloped 
with a characteristic crown of spike (S) proteins. 
ii. Spike protein: Binds ACE2 (angiotensin-converting enzyme 2) receptors on host cells, 
highly expressed in lung, heart, kidney, intestinal lining cells 

=== page 12 ===
12 
 
iii. Entry: Spike S1 binds ACE2; S2 mediates membrane fusion; TMPRSS2 primes entry 
into lung cells 
iv. Replication: Viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) replicates viral genome; 
inhibited by Remdesivir 
 
4.3.2 Transmission 
i. Airborne/aerosol: Primary route — virus-laden aerosols remain suspended for hours 
in poorly ventilated indoor spaces 
ii. Large respiratory droplets:  Short-range (<1m) transmission during coughing, 
sneezing, talking 
iii. Fomite: Indirect contact with contaminated su rfaces — less significant than initially 
thought 
iv. Incubation: 2–14 days (original strain); Omicron: 1–3 days 
v. Basic reproduction number (R₀): Original Wuhan strain ~2–3; Delta ~5–6; Omicron 
~9–12

4.3.5 Prevention & Control 
i. Vaccines: mRNA (Pfizer -BioNTech, Moderna — 90%+ efficacy against original 
strain), Viral vector (AstraZeneca, J&J), Inactivated (Covaxin, Sinovac), Protein 
subunit (Novavax) 
ii. Non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs): Masking (N95 reduces aerosol inhalation 
>90%), physical distancing, HEPA air filtration, improved ventilation 
iii. Antivirals: Paxlovid (nirmatrelvir/ritonavir) — 89% reduction in hospitalisation; 
Remdesivir for hospitalised patients; Molnupiravir 
iv. Monoclonal antibodies: Neutralising antibodies (largely ineffective against Omicron 
subvariants) 
v. Public health: Contact tracing (Aarogya Setu app in India), quarantine, border control, 
laboratory surveillance 
4.4 Biological Hazard Control 
i. Vector control:  Integrated Vector Management (IVM) combining ITNs, IRS, and 
biological control 
ii. Vaccination: Universal immunisation programmes — Expanded Programme on 
Immunisation (EPI) 
iii. WASH: Water, Sanitation and Hygiene — single most effective intervention for 
reducing waterborne and some vector-borne disease burden

iv. Surveillance: Real-time disease surveillance (IDSP, IHIP in India), genomic 
sequencing, GOARN (WHO Global Outbreak Alert and Response Network) 
v. Climate-smart health systems: Anticipate shifts in disease geography due to warming 
and plan vector control accordingly. 
 
5. Climatic Hazards 
Climatic hazards are associated with long-term changes in climate patterns, including: 
 Global warming  
 Heatwaves  
5.1 Control Strategies & Solutions 
5.1.1 Regulatory & Policy Approaches 
i. REACH Regulation (EU): Registration, Evaluation, Authorisation and Restriction of 
Chemicals — world's most comprehensive chemical law 
ii. Precautionary Principle: Restrict chemicals before definitive proof of harm — burden 
of proof on industry 
iii. WHO International Health Regulations (IHR, 2005): Binding obligat ions for member 
states to detect, report, and respond to public health emergencies 
iv. India: Environment Protection Act 1986, Drugs and Cosmetics Act, BIS standards for 
plastics in food contact, FSSAI for food additives

9. Causes of Environmental Hazards  
Environmental hazards arise due to a combination of natural processes and human 
activities. The major causes include: 
9.1 Natural Causes 
Natural processes such as tectonic mo vements, volcanic activity, and climatic variations 
can lead to environmental hazards. While these are beyond human control, their impact can 
be reduced through preparedness and planning. 
9.2 Industrialization 
Rapid industrial growth has led to increased emissions of pollutants into the air, water, and 
soil. Industries release toxic chemicals, heavy metals, and greenhouse gases, contributing 
to environmental degradation. 
9.3 Urbanization 
Unplanned urban growth leads to: 
 Overcrowding  
 Poor waste management  
 Increased pollution  

=== page 16 ===
16 
 
Urban areas often experience higher levels of air and water pollution due to vehicular 
emissions and industrial activities. 
9.4 Deforestation 
Deforestation disrupts ecosystems, reduces biodiversity, and contributes to climate change. 
It also increases the risk of soil erosion, floods, and landslides. 
9.5 Agricultural Practices 
Excessive use of fertilizers and pesticides contaminates soil and water. Irrigation practices 
can lead to salinization and waterlogging. 
9.6 Climate Change 
Climate change is a major driver of environmental hazards. It leads to: 
 Rising temperatures  
 Changing precipitation patterns  
 Increased frequency of extreme weather events  
9.7 Population Growth 
Increasing population puts pressure on natural resources, leading t o overexploitation and 
environmental degradation.

12 Summary & Key Takeaways 
Environmental hazards — chemical and biological — represent two of the most pressing 
public health and environmental challenges of the 21st century. Unders tanding their 
mechanisms enables effective prevention, regulation, and individual action. 
 
i. 4 types of environmental hazards: Physical, Chemical, Biological, Sociological — each 
requires distinct approaches 
ii. BPA is an endocrine disruptor linked to hormonal c ancers, metabolic disease, and 
neurodevelopment — regulated but still widespread 
iii. Synthetic dyes (azo group) can release carcinogenic aromatic amines; phthalates are 
anti-androgenic plasticisers — both subject to increasing regulation 
iv. Malaria: 249M cases, 608K deaths — Plasmodium falciparum via Anopheles mosquito; 
ACTs and Mosquirix vaccine are current tools 
v. COVID-19: SARS-CoV-2 pandemic revealed the speed of biological hazard spread and 
the critical need for global health equity and preparedness 
vi. Solutions: Precautionary regulation + green chemistry substitution + international 
treaties + WASH + vaccination + individual choices
