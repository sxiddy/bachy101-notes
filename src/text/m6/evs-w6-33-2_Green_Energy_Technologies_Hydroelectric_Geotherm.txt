     Green Energy Technologies: Hydroelectric and Geothermal Energy

1. Introduction

Unlike solar and wind energy, hydroelectric and geothermal energy sources are
available under different geographical conditions and are capable of offering
continuous electricity generation. Hydropower is currently the world's largest source
of renewable electricity, accounting for approximately 15-16% of global electricity
generation. Geothermal energy, while less widely deployed, provides remarkable
reliability, countries such as Iceland and Kenya generate over 20% and 47% of their
electricity from geothermal sources respectively. Understanding these technologies
is critical for any engineer working on sustainable energy systems and helps
appreciate how engineering innovation contributes to sustainable development and
energy security.

2. Hydroelectric Energy

Hydropower exploits the energy of water stored at height (potential energy) or
moving at speed (kinetic energy) to generate electricity. Ancient civilisations used
flowing rivers to operate water wheels for grinding grain; modern hydroelectric
plants use the same principle at a much larger scale.

2.1 Principle of Hydropower Energy Conversion
The energy conversion sequence in a conventional hydropower plant follows four
stages:

 The potential energy of water stored in a reservoir at elevation h is released by
   opening the sluice gates.

 This potential energy converts to kinetic energy as water flows downward
   through penstocks (large steel pipes or tunnels).

 The kinetic energy of the high-speed water jet strikes the blades of a hydraulic
   turbine, causing it to rotate and converting fluid energy into mechanical energy.

 The rotating turbine shaft drives an electrical generator, converting mechanical
   energy into electrical energy.

Thus, the overall energy transformation sequence is:

Potential Energy  Kinetic Energy  Mechanical Energy  Electrical Energy.

2.2 Major Components of a Hydroelectric Power Plant
  Reservoir -- stores large quantities of water and acts as an energy storage

     system.
  Dam -- blocks river flow, creates the reservoir, and maintains water pressure.
  Penstock -- large pipe carrying water to the turbines.
  Turbine -- rotated by flowing water, converting water energy into mechanical

     energy.

2.3 Types of Hydroelectric Power Plants
2.3.1 Storage Hydropower
Uses dams and reservoirs; the most common type worldwide, providing reliable
electricity. Example: Tehri Dam, Uttarakhand (1,000 MW).

2.3.2 Run-of-the-River Plants
Uses natural river flow without a large reservoir, resulting in a smaller
environmental impact, though electricity output varies with river discharge.
Example: Srinagar Hydro, Alaknanda River (330 MW).

2.3.3 Pumped Storage Hydropower
Functions like a giant rechargeable battery: during periods of low electricity demand,
water is pumped uphill; during peak demand, it flows downward to generate
electricity. This technology supports modern renewable energy systems by storing
excess electricity. Example: Kadamparai Pumped Storage, Tamil Nadu (400 MW).

2.3.4 Micro and Pico Hydro
Very small systems (below 100 kW) designed for remote, off-grid communities,
using small streams or canals. Example applications include rural electrification in
Himalayan villages.

2.4 Advantages of Hydroelectric Energy
    Renewable: the water cycle continuously replenishes rivers.
    Very long asset life: large hydro dams operate for 50-100+ years, giving
       extremely low long-term cost per kWh.
    Zero direct emissions during operation, though reservoir decomposition can
       emit methane in tropical regions.
    High efficiency, often exceeding 90%.

                                                                        3
    Dispatchable baseload power, unlike solar and wind, hydropower can
       generate electricity on demand at any time of day, supporting grid stability.

    Multiple co-benefits, reservoirs provide irrigation water, municipal water
       supply, flood control, navigation, fisheries, and recreation alongside power
       generation.

    Pumped storage hydro is currently the world's dominant form of grid-scale
       energy storage, essential for integrating variable renewables.

2.5 Environmental Challenges of Hydropower
    Construction of large dams floods forests and agricultural land, altering river
       ecosystems and reducing downstream fertility.
    Displacement of communities, large reservoirs flood inhabited areas; India's
       Sardar Sarovar Dam displaced an estimated 320,000 people.
    Ecosystem disruption, dams block fish migration, alter water temperature and
       sediment flow, and destroy riparian habitats.
    Sedimentation, reservoirs fill with silt over decades, reducing storage capacity
       and operational life.
    Seismic risk, very large reservoirs can induce local seismicity through
       increased water pressure on fault lines.
    Hydrology dependence, drought and changing rainfall patterns due to climate
       change can significantly reduce hydropower generation.

Engineers therefore increasingly focus on environmentally sustainable dam designs
to mitigate these concerns.

2.6 Hydropower in India
India possesses enormous hydroelectric potential due to the Himalayan rivers and
the Western Ghats. Major hydroelectric projects include the Bhakra Nangal Project,
Hirakud Dam, Tehri Dam, and Sardar Sarovar Project, which provide electricity
while also supporting irrigation and water management. Small hydropower projects

3. Geothermal Energy

The word "geothermal" comes from two Greek words, geo (Earth) and therme (heat).
Geothermal energy is the heat stored beneath the Earth's surface, originating from
the planet's formation and the natural decay of radioactive elements inside it. Unlike
solar and wind energy, geothermal energy is available continuously, operating day
and night throughout the year, which makes it an excellent source of base-load
electricity.

3.1 Sources of Geothermal Heat
Earth's interior contains extremely high temperatures, and this temperature increases
with depth, a phenomenon known as the geothermal gradient. This heat reaches
underground water reservoirs, producing hot water or steam. Engineers drill wells
to access these geothermal reservoirs, and the resulting steam drives turbines
connected to electrical generators.

3.2 How Geothermal Power Plants Work
Production wells bring hot water or steam to the surface. This steam rotates turbines,
which drive generators to produce electricity. After cooling, the water is injected
back underground through injection wells, a recycling process that helps maintain
reservoir pressure and long-term sustainability.

3.3 Types of Geothermal Power Plants
3.3.1 Dry Steam Plants
The simplest and oldest type, in which natural dry steam from underground
reservoirs is piped directly to turbines. The Geysers in California, the world's largest
geothermal complex, operates on dry steam. This type is comparatively rare, as few
reservoirs naturally produce dry steam rather than hot water.

3.3.2 Flash Steam Plants

                                                                        5
The most common geothermal technology globally. Hot, pressurised water above
180°C from deep wells is rapidly depressurised ("flashed") in surface flash tanks,
causing part of it to instantly vaporise into steam. This steam drives the turbines,
while the remaining liquid water is re-injected into the reservoir.

3.3.3 Binary Cycle Plants
Used for lower-temperature resources (100-180°C). Moderately hot geothermal
water heats a secondary working fluid with a lower boiling point (such as isobutane
or pentane) through a heat exchanger. The secondary fluid vaporises and drives the
turbine, while the geothermal water never contacts the turbine and is fully re-
injected. This is the most flexible plant type, as it can exploit lower-temperature
reservoirs unusable by flash or dry steam plants, and produces almost zero
atmospheric emissions.

3.4 Applications of Geothermal Energy
    Electricity generation
    District heating
    Greenhouse heating
    Industrial drying
    Aquaculture
    Hot water supply
    Spa tourism
    Heat pumps for buildings

3.5 Advantages of Geothermal Energy
    Baseload power, geothermal plants operate continuously with capacity factors
       of 80-95%, far exceeding solar (20-25%) and wind (25-40%); Iceland's
       geothermal plants run virtually year-round.
    Tiny land footprint compared to other renewable technologies for the same
       energy output.

                                                                        6
    Minimal emissions, binary cycle plants produce virtually zero direct
       emissions; even flash steam plants emit only small amounts of naturally
       occurring gases.

    Stable, predictable output, unaffected by weather, season, or time of day.
    Long resource life, properly managed reservoirs can sustain output for

       decades if extraction rates balance natural recharge.
    Available 24 hours a day, reduces fossil fuel dependence, and is well suited

       for sustainable cities.

3.6 Limitations of Geothermal Energy
    Geographic specificity, economical resources are concentrated in geologically
       active regions; India has limited high-temperature hydrothermal resources
       compared to volcanic nations.
    High upfront exploration costs, deep drilling is expensive and risky, as wells
       may not encounter productive reservoirs after significant investment.
    Enhanced Geothermal System (EGS) technical challenges, induced seismicity
       from hydraulic fracturing is a concern; an EGS project in Basel, Switzerland
       was abandoned after causing felt earthquakes.
    Hydrogen sulphide emissions, some hydrothermal systems release hydrogen
       sulphide gas, requiring treatment to prevent air pollution and health impacts.

Modern technologies and management practices are steadily reducing these risks.

3.7 Geothermal Energy Around the World
Countries with significant geothermal development include Iceland, the United
States, Indonesia, the Philippines, Kenya, and New Zealand. Iceland is particularly
remarkable, with nearly all buildings receiving geothermal heating, substantially
reducing dependence on fossil fuels.

3.8 Geothermal Energy in India
India possesses moderate geothermal potential. Important geothermal regions
include Puga Valley (Ladakh), Tattapani (Chhattisgarh), Manikaran (Himachal

8. Summary
Hydroelectric energy converts the energy of flowing water into electricity through
turbines and generators. It is reliable, efficient, and widely used, although careful
environmental management is essential. Geothermal energy harnesses the Earth's
internal heat to produce electricity and provide heating. It is available continuously
with very low emissions but depends on suitable geological conditions. Together
with solar and wind energy, these technologies form the backbone of the global
transition toward sustainable energy systems. As engineers, the challenge is not only
to generate energy, but to generate it responsibly, efficiently, and sustainably.
