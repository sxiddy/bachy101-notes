                Green Energy Technologies: Solar and Wind Energy

1. Introduction

The world currently depends on fossil fuels, coal, oil, and natural gas; for over 80%
of its total energy supply. These fuels have powered industrial growth for two
centuries, but their environmental consequences, particularly greenhouse gas
emissions, are now well documented. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate
Change (IPCC) has set a global target of net-zero emissions by 2050. India, the
world's third-largest emitter of greenhouse gases, has committed under the Paris
Agreement to achieving 50% of its cumulative electricity installed capacity from
non-fossil fuel sources by 2030, backed by a national target of 500 GW of renewable
energy capacity by 2030, with solar and wind identified as the two primary pillars
of this transition.

This lecture focuses on solar and wind energy, the two fastest-growing and most
rapidly maturing segments of the renewable energy portfolio, and examines their
working principles, technologies, applications, advantages, and limitations from an
engineering perspective.

2. Green Energy: Concepts and Definitions

Renewable energy refers to energy derived from natural processes that replenish
faster than they are consumed; sunlight, wind, flowing water, geothermal heat, and
biomass growth. Green energy is a broader, less technical term that generally refers
to renewable sources with minimal environmental impact across their entire
lifecycle, production, operation, and disposal.

2.1 Characteristics of Green Energy

    Sustainable
    Clean and low-carbon
    Environmentally friendly
    Locally available

Note: not all renewable energy is automatically "green" in every sense. For instance,
large hydroelectric dams are renewable but can have significant ecological
consequences, and biomass combustion, while renewable, still produces emissions.
This lecture focuses on two genuinely green sources: solar energy (photovoltaic and
thermal) and wind energy (onshore and offshore).

3. Solar Energy

Solar energy technology converts electromagnetic radiation from the sun into a
usable form of energy, most commonly electricity. The sun delivers approximately
1,000 watts per square metre of solar irradiance to the Earth's surface at solar noon

on a clear day. This value is the baseline used in every solar system output

3.2 Approaches to Harnessing Solar Energy
3.2.1 Photovoltaic (PV) Systems
Photovoltaic systems convert sunlight directly into electricity using the photovoltaic
effect. When photons strike a semiconductor material, typically crystalline silicon,
they excite electrons enough to knock them loose from their atomic bonds. The cell
is engineered with a p-n junction, a boundary between two differently doped silicon
layers, which creates an internal electric field. This field sweeps the freed electrons
in one direction, generating a direct current. This is solid-state energy conversion,
involving no moving parts, no combustion, and no working fluid.

3.2.2 Solar Thermal Systems
Solar thermal systems use mirrors or lenses to concentrate sunlight and generate
heat, which can drive a conventional steam turbine or be used directly for heating
water or buildings. Concentrated Solar Power (CSP) plants use this principle at
utility scale, often paired with a thermal storage medium such as molten salt, which
addresses solar's core weakness the fact that it does not generate power at night.

3.3 Types of Solar PV Technology

                                                                      3
3.3.1 Monocrystalline Silicon Cells
Cut from a single continuous crystal structure using the energy-intensive
Czochralski process. They are the most efficient commercially available option,
typically reaching 20-22% efficiency, with a uniform dark appearance, but are more
expensive per watt.

3.3.2 Polycrystalline Silicon Cells
Made from multiple silicon fragments melted together. They are cheaper to produce
but slightly less efficient (typically 15-17%), because grain boundaries between
crystals impede electron flow.

3.3.3 Thin-Film Technologies
Includes cadmium telluride and amorphous silicon cells, which use much less
semiconductor material deposited in very thin layers on a substrate. They are lighter,
more flexible, and cheaper, but generally less efficient (10-13%). They suit
applications where weight or flexibility matters more than maximising energy
density, such as building-integrated photovoltaics.

3.3.4 Perovskite Solar Cells (Emerging Technology)
Perovskite cells have seen laboratory efficiencies climb rapidly over the past decade
and can potentially be layered with silicon cells in a "tandem" configuration to push
combined efficiencies above 30%. They are cheaper to manufacture but currently
face durability and stability challenges, degrading faster than silicon when exposed
to moisture and UV light: an example of a technology that is scientifically exciting
but not yet engineering-ready at scale.

3.5 Challenges of Solar Energy
    High upfront cost for panels, inverters, and installation
    Weather dependency and intermittency, output varies with cloud cover, time
       of day, and season
    Degradation over time, panels typically lose about 0.5% of output capacity
       per year, retaining roughly 80-85% of original output after 25 years
    Orientation and tracking requirements, fixed-tilt systems are cheaper but
       sacrifice output; single- and dual-axis tracking systems can increase yield by
       15-35% at the cost of added complexity and maintenance
    Storage expense: costly battery systems are needed for 24/7 use
    Space-intensive: requires large land or roof area for high output
    Materials and end-of-life management: manufacturing silicon cells is energy-
       intensive and produces hazardous by-products; recycling and disposal of
       panels at scale is an emerging engineering and policy challenge

3.6 Solar Energy in India
India receives approximately 300 sunny days annually, making it highly suitable for
solar energy generation. The Government of India has introduced several policies
and financial incentives to achieve its target of 500 GW of renewable energy
capacity by 2030.

4. Wind Energy

Wind energy draws on fluid dynamics rather than semiconductor physics, but shares
many of the same systems-engineering challenges as solar. Wind originates because
the sun heats the Earth's surface unevenly, creating air pressure differences; this
moving air, or wind, carries kinetic energy that wind turbines convert into electricity.

4.1 Working Principle of a Wind Turbine
Wind strikes the turbine blades, causing them to rotate. This rotates the rotor, and a
gearbox increases the rotational speed delivered to the generator, which produces
electricity. This electricity is then fed into the electrical grid.

4.3 Types of Wind Turbines
4.3.1 Horizontal Axis Wind Turbine (HAWT)
The most common design worldwide, offering high efficiency.

4.3.2 Vertical Axis Wind Turbine (VAWT)
Suitable for urban areas and capable of receiving wind from different directions.

4.4 Wind Farms: Onshore and Offshore
A single turbine produces limited electricity, so large numbers of turbines are
installed together to form a wind farm, which may be located onshore or offshore.
Offshore wind farms access stronger, steadier winds and avoid many land-use
conflicts and noise concerns, but involve dramatically higher installation and
maintenance costs due to the marine environment, foundation engineering
challenges, and undersea cabling logistics. Floating offshore turbine platforms are
an active area of engineering development, enabling deployment in deeper waters
than fixed-foundation designs allow.

Wind turbines have a higher capacity factor than solar -- typically 35-50% onshore
and up to 50-60% offshore, because turbines can generate around the clock
whenever wind conditions allow. However, wind systems have more moving parts,
higher maintenance requirements, and face more significant siting and permitting
hurdles.

4.8 Wind Energy Potential and Initiatives in India

India's eastern coastline is prone to cyclones, making the western coast more
attractive for wind energy development. India has a coastline of about 7,516.6 km

Five major wind energy initiatives in India:

 National Wind-Solar Hybrid Policy (2018) -- promotes combined use of wind
   and solar energy, better land and transmission utilisation, and conversion of
   existing plants into hybrid facilities.

 National Offshore Wind Energy Policy (2015) -- harnesses offshore wind
   potential along the coastline, with the Ministry of New and Renewable Energy
   (MNRE) as the nodal ministry, focused on states such as Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.

 Renewable Purchase Obligation (RPO) -- mandates distribution companies
   and large consumers to procure a specified share of electricity from renewable
   sources, including wind.

 Green Energy Corridor Programme -- strengthens transmission infrastructure
   to integrate wind and solar electricity into the national grid, reducing losses and
   congestion.

 Production Linked Incentive (PLI) Scheme for Renewable Energy
   Manufacturing -- promotes domestic manufacturing of renewable energy
   equipment under the "Make in India" initiative.

5. Comparing Solar and Wind Energy

Solar energy uses sunlight and works during daylight hours; it is silent, allows
rooftop installation, requires lower maintenance, and is ideal in sunny regions. Wind
energy uses moving air, works whenever wind speed is sufficient, produces
moderate noise, requires higher maintenance and open windy areas, and is ideal in
coastal and hilly regions. Wind systems generally achieve a higher capacity factor

                                                                      9
than solar because turbines can generate around the clock, but they involve more
moving parts and greater siting and permitting complexity.

Importantly, solar and wind resources are often complementary in time -- solar
output peaks at midday, while wind is often stronger at night or during storm systems
-- which is why grid planners increasingly favour developing both technologies
together rather than relying on a single source. The global energy transition is
fundamentally a systems-engineering challenge involving materials science, fluid
dynamics, power electronics, structural engineering, and policy working together.
