AIR POLLUTION 
1. Introduction  
Air is essential for life, yet its quality is rapidly declining due to increasing human 
activities. Air pollution is often invisible, but its effects are widespread —from 
respiratory diseases to global warming.  
It is one of the most critical environmental challenges affecting both human 
health and the planet. 
2. Definition of Air Pollution  
“The presence of one or more harmful substances in the atmosphere in 
concentrations high enough to cause damage to living organisms and the 
environment.” 
These harmful substances are called pollutants, which may be gases, particles, 
or biological materials. Eg., dust, smoke. etc. 
Key idea: 
• Clean air has a balanced composition  78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, argon 
less than 1%, carbon di-oxide, water vapour and trace amounts of ozone, 
helium, ammonia. 
• Pollution occurs when this balance is disturbed  
• World Health organization ( WHO) defines air pollution as the 
contamination of indoor or outdoor environments by any chemical, 
physical, or biological agent that modifies the natural characteristics of the 
atmosphere and poses risks to human health and the environment. 
3. Key Statistics tell us that: 
i. 7 million premature deaths annually attributed to air pollution (WHO, 2023) 
ii. 99% of the world population breathes air that exceeds WHO quality guideline 
limits 
iii. Air pollution costs the global economy over 5 trillion annually in welfare 
losses 
iv. Delhi, Lahore, and Dhaka consistently rank among world's most polluted 
cities (AQI > 300 in winters)

The Air quality index  is a standardized tool used by government agencies to 
measure and report daily air pollution levels, usually on a scale from 0 to 500. It 
transforms complex measurements of 5 -8 key pollutants into a simple number, 
colour code and health descriptor, indicating how clean or polluted the air is. 
i. If the AQI range is 0 -50, it is categorized as good with a colour code of 
green and the health advisory tells that the air quality is satisfactory. 
ii. 51-100, the colour code is yellow, the air quality is acceptable; however, 
some sensitive groups are affected. 
iii. 101-200, unhealthy, orange is the colour code and sensitive groups should 
limit outdoor activity. 
iv. 201-300, unhealthy, red is the colour code, everyone in the vicinity gets 
affected. 
v. 301-400, very unhealthy, purple is the colour code, health alert, and to 
avoid outdoor activity. 
vi. If the AQI is between 401 and 500, it is categorized as hazardous, the 
colour code is maroon and heath advisory calls it as emergency conditions 
for the whole population. 
4. Different types of air pollution 
4.1 Based on origin 
4.1.1 Primary Pollutants 
i. Primary air pollutants are those that are directly emitted into the atmosphere 
in harmful form from identifiable sources.  
Some examples of primary air pollutants. 
ii. Carbon Monoxide (CO): Colourless, odourless gas from incomplete 
combustion. Binds haemoglobin 200× stronger than O₂, causing hypoxia.  
iii. Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂): From coal combustion in power plants, volcanic 
eruptions. Causes acid rain and respiratory damage. 
iv. Nitrogen Oxides (NOₓ): From high-temperature combustion (vehicles, power 
plants). Precursor to smog and acid rain. 
v. Particulate Matter (PM 2.5 / PM 10): Fine particles from dust, combustion, 
construction. PM2.5 penetrates deep into lungs and bloodstream. 
vi. Volatile Organic Compounds (VOCs): From paints, solvents, vehicle exhaust. 
Precursors to ground-level ozone. 
vii. Ammonia (NH₃): Primarily from agriculture (fertilizers, livestock). 
Contributes to PM2.5 formation. 
viii. Lead (Pb): From leaded fuels (now largely phased out), industrial processes. 
Neurotoxin.

4.1.1 Secondary Pollutants 
• Secondary pollutants  are NOT directly emitted, but  form through 
atmospheric reactions.  
• Ground-level ozone (O₃):  
NOₓ + VOCs + sunlight → O₃. Major component of photochemical smog. 
Damages respiratory tissue. 
• Peroxyacetyl nitrates (PAN):  
VOCs + NOₓ + sunlight. Causes eye irritation. Found in photochemical 
smog. 
Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) / Nitric acid (HNO₃):  
• SO₂/NOₓ + water vapour → acid rain. 
• Secondary Particulate Matter: Sulphates, nitrates, and organic carbon formed 
from gas-phase precursors. 
• Important: Secondary pollutants often result from chemical reactions and can 
be more harmful. 
4.2 Based on Physical Form 
4.2.1. Particulate Matter (PM) 
i. Includes dust, smoke, soot  
ii. PM2.5 is especially dangerous as it enters deep into lungs  
4.2.2. Gaseous Pollutants 
i. Carbon dioxide (CO₂)  
ii. Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂)  
iii. Sulphur dioxide (SO₂)  
 
4.3. Indoor vs Outdoor Pollution 
• Outdoor: vehicles, industries, construction  
• Indoor: cooking fuels, poor ventilation –they are primary air pollutants. 
WHO Fact:  Indoor air pollution causes approximately 3.8 million deaths 
annually more than outdoor pollution in many developing nations. Women 
cooking on biomass stoves are disproportionately affected.

i. Radon (Rn): Radioactive gas from soil/rock; seeps into buildings. Second

ii. Formaldehyde: From pressed wood, insulation, tobacco smoke. IARC Group

iii. Tobacco smoke: Contains 7 0+carcinogens including benzene, arsenic,

5. Causes of Air Pollution  
5.1. Natural Causes  
i. Volcanic eruptions: Massive SO₂ and particulate emissions (e.g., Mount 
Pinatubo, 1991) 
ii. Forest fires: CO, PM2.5, black carbon, ozone precursors 
iii. Dust storms:  PM10 from deserts (Sahara, Thar Desert contribute to 
regional haze) 
iv. Biogenic VOCs : Isoprene from forests contributes to natural ozone 
formation 
5.2. Human-Made Causes  
5.2.1. Industrial Emissions 
i. Coal-fired thermal power plants: Largest emitters of SO₂, NOₓ, and mercury 
ii. Cement industry: Dust emissions, CO₂ (produces ~8% of global CO₂) 
iii. Steel industry: Coke ovens emit SO₂, PAHs, benzene 
iv. Brick kilns: Major source of PM and black carbon in South Asia 
v. Chemical & petroleum refineries: SO₂, H₂S, VOCs, benzene 
• Release toxic gases and chemicals

5.2.2. Vehicular Emissions 
i. Major urban contributor  
ii. Internal combustion engines emit CO, NOₓ, hydrocarbons (HC), and PM2.5 
iii. Diesel vehicles : highest PM 2.5 emitter; black carbon (soot) major 
contributor 
iv. Two-wheelers in India run on older, inefficient engines significant VOC 
emitters 
v. Aviation: Jet fuel combustion emits NOₓ and black carbon at high altitudes 
vi. India: Transport sector contributes ~30% of urban air pollution 
 
5.2.3. Burning of Fossil Fuels 
• Coal and petroleum release CO₂ and SO₂  
5.2.4. Agricultural Activities 
• Stubble/crop residue burning (Punjab, Haryana): Major contributor to 
Delhi winter smog 
• NH₃ from fertilizer application and livestock manure 
• Paddy field methane (CH₄) — potent greenhouse gas 
• Pesticide spraying releases fine chemical aerosols 
5.2.5. Construction and Dust 
• Urban dust pollution  
5.2.6. Waste Burning 
• Releases toxic substances  
Key takeaway: Human activities are the dominant cause of modern air pollution. 
 
6. Effects of Air Pollution  
6.1. Effects on Human Health 
6.1.1. Respiratory System

i. Asthma: PM 2.5 and O₃ trigger bronchoconstriction; prevalence increasing 
globally 
ii. COPD (Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease): Long -term exposure to 
smoke and PM 
iii. Lung cancer: PM 2.5 and carcinogens (PAHs, benzene) are IARC Group 1 
carcinogens 
iv. Reduced lung function in children exposed to pollution during development 
v. Pneumonia: Leading infectious killer, exacerbated by weakened

6.1.2. Cardiovascular System 
i. Ischemic heart disease: PM 2.5 enters bloodstream, causes inflammation and 
atherosclerosis 
ii. Stroke: NO₂ and fine particle exposure associated with increased stroke risk 
iii. Hypertension: Long-term exposure causes systemic inflammation and blood 
pressure rise 
iv. Cardiac arrhythmias: Short-term PM spikes trigger irregular heart rhythms 
 
6.1.3. Neurological & Other Effects 
i. Emerging evidence: PM2.5 linked to cognitive decline and Alzheimer's disease 
ii. Lead and mercury: Neurotoxins causing developmental problems in children 
iii. Pre-term birth and low birth weight from maternal air pollution exposure 
iv. Diabetes: Chronic PM2.5 exposure associated with insulin resistance 
v. Eye and skin irritation from O₃, SO₂, and particulates 
• Lung cancer  
    Fine particles (PM2.5) are especially dangerous. 
 
6.2. Environmental Effects 
6.2.1. Global Warming and Climate Change 

=== page 7 ===
7 
 
Greenhouse gases (CO₂, CH₄, N₂O) trap infrared radiation in the atmosphere and 
increases the earth’s temperature. The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (2021) 
confirms 1.1°C warming since the pre -industrial era. Projected impacts include 
sea level rise of 0.28 –1.01 m by 2100, more frequent extreme weather, and 
ecosystem disruption. 
6.2.2. Acid Rain 
• Damages crops, soil, and buildings. 
SO₂ and NOₓ react with water vapour and oxygen to form sulphuric and nitric 
acids. Acid rain (pH < 5.6) damages forests, corrodes limestone buildings and 
monuments (e.g., Taj Mahal yellowing), and acidifies lakes — killing fish and 
aquatic life. The 'Black Forest' (Schwarzwald) damage in Germany in the 1970s–
80s is a historical example. 
6.2.3. Ozone Layer Depletion 
• Increases harmful UV radiation  
Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) from refrigerants and aerosols release Cl radicals in 
the stratosphere that catalytically destroy O₃. The Antarctic ozone hole was 
discovered in 1985. The Montreal Protocol (1987) successfully phased out CFCs 
considered the most successful international environmental agreement. 
6.2.4 Photochemical Smog 
• Reduces visibility and affects health  
VOCs + NOₓ + sunlight → O₃ + PAN + secondary aerosols.  
First observed in Los Angeles in the 1940s. Delhi's winter smog is worsened by 
temperature inversion trapping pollutants near ground level. Visibility drops 
below 500 m; flights are cancelled; respiratory emergencies surge.

7. Control Measures  
7.1. Technological Solutions 
Particulate matter (PM) removal from exhaust gases is achieved through 
technologies like baghouses (fabric filters), electrostatic precipitators (ESPs), 
scrubbers, and cyclones. These systems utilize filtration, electrical charging, 
centrifugal force, or s crubbing liquid to capture dust and ash, with ESPs and 
fabric filters capable of over 99% efficiency in industrial settings 
7.1.1 Particulate Control Technologies 
 
i. Fabric filters or baghouses:  Dust-laden gases are passed through dense 
fabric bags, cake like formation filters the dust off the gases efficiently. Used 
specifically for fine, sub-micron particles. 
 
They  are industrial air pollution control devices designed to remove particulate 
matter and dust from commercial exhaust gas streams.

How Baghouses Work 
1. Air Intake: Dust-laden gas enters through the dirty-air inlet into the dirty air 
chamber. 
2. Filtration: The gas passes through long, cylindrical filter bags made of woven 
or felted fabric. Dust particles are trapped on the outer surface of the bags, 
forming a "dust cake" that aids in fine filtration. 
3. Clean Air Discharge: Filtered air continues into the clean air chamber at the 
top and exits via the clean-air outlet. 
4. Cleaning Cycle: Over time, accumulated dust causes pressure drop. A pulse-jet 
cleaning system sends short blasts of compressed air down into the bags, 
expanding them rapidly and shaking the dust cake loose. 
5. Dust Collection: Shaken-off particles drop into the bottom hopper and exit 
through a rotary airlock valve for disposal or recycling. 
 
ii. Electrostatic precipitators: Use an electrostatic charge to attract particulate 
matter onto the collector electrodes, removing the fine ash and soot from 
industrial exhaust. 
iii. Wet scrubbers:  Use a scrubbing liquid (usually water) to trap particles, 
making them suitable for high-temperature or moisture-laden gases, as well as 
handling both solid particles and gaseous pollutants. 
iv. Cyclonic separators: Use a centrifugal force, often in a vortex (cyclone) to 
separate heavy particulates from gas streams, commonly used for larger 
particles. 
v. Gravity settling chambers: Use gravity to allow larger particles to settle out 
of the gas stream, often used as pre-cleaners for other devices. 
vi. Cleaner fuels (LPG, CNG, renewables)  
vii. Electric vehicles  
 
7.1.2. Gaseous Pollutant Control 
i. Flue Gas Desulphurization (FGD): Limestone scrubbers remove SO₂ from 
power plant exhaust. Now mandatory for Indian thermal plants. 
ii. Selective Catalytic Reduction (SCR): Ammonia injection + catalyst converts 
NOₓ to N₂ and H₂O. 
iii. Catalytic Converters (in vehicles): Oxidize CO and HC; reduce NOₓ. Essential 
for BS-VI compliance. 
iv. Activated Carbon Adsorption: Captures VOCs and organic air pollutants.

7.2. Government Measures 
7.2.1 Policy & Regulatory Controls 
i. Bharat Stage (BS) Emission Standards: India adopted BS -VI in 2020 — 
equivalent to Euro 6. Cuts NOₓ by 70% from BS-IV. 
ii. Electric Vehicle (EV) Policy: FAME II Scheme promotes EV adoption. PLI 
scheme for battery manufacturing. 
iii. Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP): Delhi — emergency controls at AQI 
> 200 (ban construction, brick kilns, diesel trucks) 
iv. National Clean Air Programme (NCAP, 2019): Target 20 –30% reduction in 
PM2.5 and PM10 by 2024 in 122 non-attainment cities 
7.2.2 Renewable Energy & Transition 
i. Solar energy: India targets 500 GW renewable capacity by 2030 
ii. Wind energy: Offshore and onshore wind replacing coal capacity 
iii. Energy efficiency: BEE Star Labels, LED lighting, green building codes 
iv. International Solar Alliance (ISA) — India-led global initiative 
7.2.3 Green Infrastructure 
i. Urban tree planting — trees absorb CO₂, PM; provide cooling 
ii. Green roofs and walls reduce urban heat island effect 
iii. Constructed wetlands for industrial emission zone buffers 
iv. Industrial zones located downwind from residential areas (zoning laws) 
7.2.4 India's Legal Framework 
i. Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1981: Establishes State 
Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs) and emission standards 
ii. Environment Protection Act, 1986: Umbrella legislation allowing the central 
government to take measures to protect the environment 
iii. Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB): Sets National Ambient Air Quality 
Standards (NAAQS); monitors compliance 
iv. NAAQS Standards: PM2.5 — 40 µg/m³ (annual); PM 10 — 60 µg/m³ (annual) 
[note: WHO guidelines are stricter: 5 and 15 µg/m³] 
v. SAFAR System: Real -time air quality monitoring and forecasting across 
major cities

8: Case study 
8.1 London Great Smog (1952) 
A combination of fog, coal smoke, and temperature inversion created a lethal 
smog blanket over London for 5 days in December 1952. An estimated 4,000 
people died immediately; long-term deaths reached 12,000. This disaster directly 
led to the UK's Clean Air  Act of 1956 — one of the world's first air pollution 
control laws. 
8.2 Delhi Winter Smog 
Every winter, Delhi experiences severe smog episodes driven by stubble burning 
from Punjab and Haryana, vehicle emissions, construction dust, and cold 
temperatures causing temperature inversion. AQI regularly exceeds 400. GRAP 
measures including odd-even vehicle rationing, school closures, and ban on diesel 
generators are triggered as emergency responses. 
9. Conclusion  
Air pollution is a serious but manageable problem. While industrialization and 
urbanization have increased pollution levels, solutions are available at every 
level—from governments to individuals. 
As students and future professionals, your role is crucial in: 
• Promoting sustainable practices  
• Developing cleaner technologies  
• Creating awareness  
Remember: Clean air is essential for life, not a privilege.
