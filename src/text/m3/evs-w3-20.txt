1. Introduction 
1.1 Session Overview 
This session addresses a topic directly relevant to the world around us: Circular 
Economy and Solid Waste Management. It builds a clear mental model of how waste 
is generated, how it can be managed, and how engineering thinking can transform 
waste into a resource.

2. Solid Waste: What It Is and Why It Matters 
2.1 Definition of Solid Waste 
Solid waste, at its simplest, is any discarded material that is solid or semi -solid 
anything we throw away or no longer want. However, not all waste is the same, and 
this matters enormously for how we manage it as an engineering challenge. 
2.2 Classification of Municipal Solid Waste 
iv. Municipal Solid Waste (MSW):  Household garbage : food scraps, paper, 
plastics, glass. The category most people deal with every day. 
v. Industrial Waste: By-products of manufacturing, such as slag from a steel plant, 
ash from a thermal power station, and chemical residues. 
vi. Biomedical Waste: Generated by hospitals and clinics: used syringes, bandages, 
expired medicines. Demands very special handling because of infection risks. 
vii. E-waste: Discarded electronics such as old phones or broken laptops. Often 
contains heavy metals like lead and mercury, which are highly toxic if they leach 
into soil or water. 
viii. Construction and Demolition Waste: Concrete, bricks, and wood from building 
sites. 
ix. Agricultural Waste: Crop residues and manure from farming. 
2.3 Why Classification Matters 
A one-size-fits-all approach to waste management does not work. Biomedical waste 
cannot go into the same stream as vegetable peels, and e-waste cannot be burnt in a 
backyard pit because the fumes are toxic. Each category needs a tailored engineering 
solution. 
Engagement check:  Food waste actually makes up about 50 –60% of Indian 
household MSW, which has major implications for how waste systems are designed. 
3. Linear to Circular Economy 
3.1 The Linear Economy Model 
For the last two hundred years, the global economy has worked on a simple model: 
raw materials are taken from the earth- iron ore, timber, petroleum, something useful 
is made, people use it, and then it is disposed of. This is the linear economy, often 
described in three words: take, make, dispose.

This model made sense when the world's population was small, resources seemed 
infinite, and the environment could absorb whatever was discarded. None of those 
conditions hold true today. Globally, about 100 billion tonnes of materials are used 
every year, and less than 9% of it ever comes back into productive use , the rest is 
buried in landfills, burnt, or leaked into ecosystems. 
“The question is no longer how we dispose of waste, but how we keep resources in 
use for as long as possible.” 
If the linear economy follows the path of ‘take, make, and dispose,’ the circular 
economy follows the path of ‘reduce, reuse, recycle, and regenerate.’ 
3.2 The Circular Economy Model 
The circular economy is the alternative to a line that ends in a rubbish dump. Imagine 
instead a loop, a cycle where materials keep flowing. Products are designed so that 
at the end of their useful life, the materials can come back and be used again. Waste 
from one process becomes the raw material for another. 
3.3 Three Core Principles of the Circular Economy 
i. Design out waste and pollution from the very beginning , at the product design 
stage, before a single unit is manufactured. 
ii. Keep products and materials in use for as long as possible , through repair, 
remanufacturing, and refurbishment. 
iii. Regenerate natural systems, actively restore the environment rather than simply 
avoid harming it, for example by returning nutrients to the soil through 
composting. 
3.4 Economic Significance 
The circular economy is not just an environmental idea; it is an economic one. The 
Ellen MacArthur Foundation estimates this shift could create USD 4.5 trillion in 
global economic benefit by 2030. This represents an engineering and business 
opportunity, not charity. 
3.5 Linear vs Circular: A Comparative Example 
In the linear economy, a glass bottle is used once and goes to landfill. In the circular 
economy, that same bottle is collected, cleaned, and refilled, potentially hundreds of 
times, before the glass itself is eventually recycled. Every loop saves raw materials, 
energy, and money. 
“Circular thinking starts at the drawing board, not at the rubbish bin.”

When engineers design a product, a building, or a system, they are not just solving 
a problem for today: they are deciding what happens to those materials in 5, 10, or 
50 years. 
4. The 3Rs and Waste Treatment Methods 
4.1 The 3Rs Hierarchy 
The 3Rs: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, are the operational heart of the circular economy, 
and they are listed in a deliberate order of priority. Reduce is first because it solves 
the problem before it exists. Reuse is second because it extends the life of something 
already made. Recycle is third , it is important, but it still requires energy and 
produces some leftover waste, making it the last resort among the three, not the first 
action. 
4.1.1 Reduce 
Reduce means generating less waste in the first place. In engineering, this shows up 
as dematerialization : designing products that achieve the same function with less 
material. It also shows up in policy through Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) 
schemes, where manufacturers are legally responsible for what happens to their 
product at end of life : an incentive that changes how products are designed. 
4.1.2 Reuse 
Reuse means using something again without significant reprocessing. The classic 
Indian example is the steel tiffin box, used daily for decades, in contrast with single-
use plastic containers. The tiffin box is built on reuse principles by design. 
4.1.3 Recycle 
Recycle means processing material into something new. The central challenge is 
contamination; a paper carton soaked in curry cannot be recycled. This is why source 
segregation, separating wet waste from dry waste at home, is not just a good habit 
but a technical necessity for recycling to work at scale. 
5 Waste Treatment Methods: Overview 
When waste cannot be reduced, reused, or recycled, engineering methods are needed 
to treat or dispose of it safely. There are four main methods: composting, anaerobic 
digestion, incineration, and sanitary landfill.

5.1 Composting 
Organic waste: food scraps, garden material , is broken down by microorganisms 
under controlled conditions. The result is compost: a nutrient-rich material that goes 
back into the soil, closing the loop on organic matter completely. Vermicomposting, 
which uses earthworms, is a variant that works well even at household scale. 
5.2 Anaerobic Digestion 
Similar to composting, but carried out without oxygen in a closed chamber. 
Microorganisms decompose the organic material and produce biogas ; mainly 
methane, which can be used for cooking or generating electricity. The leftover 
digestate serves as a fertilizer, so this single process yields both energy and a nutrient 
product. 
5.2.1 Advantages: 
i. Adding manure to soil increases water retention and ion-exchange capacity. 
ii. A number of industrial solid wastes can also be treated by this method. 
iii. Manure can be sold, reducing the cost of disposing of the wastes. 
iv. Recycling is effective. 
 
5.2.2 Disadvantages: 
i. Non-consumables have to be disposed of separately. 
ii. Use of compost has not yet caught up with farmers, so there is no assured market. 
5.3 Incineration 
Controlled burning of waste at very high temperatures : between 800 and 1000 
degrees Celsius. It reduces waste volume by up to 90%. Modern waste -to-energy 
plants capture the heat to generate electricity; an incinerator plant of 300 tonnes per 
day capacity can generate 3 MW of power. The main concern is air pol lution from 
toxic compounds like dioxins, so emission control systems are non -negotiable, and 
the ash residue also requires careful disposal.

5.4 Sanitary Landfill 
The engineered version of a rubbish dump. The base is lined with impermeable 
material to stop toxic leachate from reaching groundwater. Landfill gas : methane 
from buried organic matter: is captured and can be used as energy. Landfills take up 
enormous amounts of land and represent the least -value option in the waste 
hierarchy; the goal of good waste management is to send as little as possible to 
landfill.

6. Integrated Solid Waste Management (ISWM) 
No single method handles all types of waste. Real cities and municipalities use 
Integrated Solid Waste Management a system that combines all these approaches. 
The hierarchy is: prevent first, then reuse, then recycle, then compost or digest, then 
recover energy through incineration, and only then, as a last resort, landfill. 
In India, the Solid Waste Management Rules of 2016 made source segregation 
mandatory and required bulk waste generators: hotels, hospitals, market complexes: 
to set up their own decentralized processing. This policy framework mirrors the 
ISWM philosophy; implementation is still uneven, but the regulatory intent is now 
firmly circular. 
7. Real-World Applications and Challenges 
7.1 Kalundborg, Denmark: Industrial Symbiosis 
A cluster of industries exchange each other's by-products: a power plant's steam goes 
to a pharmaceutical factory, its fly ash goes to a cement plant, and its sulphur by -
product goes to a wallboard manufacturer. None of those outputs go to waste: a clear 
example of industrial symbiosis and circular thinking at an industrial scale. 
7.2 Thiruvananthapuram Biogas Plant, Kerala 
This plant converts daily market waste: vegetable and fruit scraps: into biogas used 
for cooking. A city's food waste becomes its energy: the circular economy working 
in an Indian context, at community scale. 
7.3 Urban Mining in Japan 
Urban mining programs in Japan extract gold, silver, and rare earth elements from 
discarded electronics, often at concentrations higher than natural ore deposits. Japan 
calls this the “urban mine” : it is both financially valuable and reduces the need for 
destructive mining. A city in Japan has also been marked as a zero-waste city. 
7.4 Challenges in Implementation 
i. Advanced recycling and Waste -to-Energy (WtE) technologies need significant 
capital investment that many municipalities do not have. 
ii. Recycling economics are sensitive to commodity prices: when virgin plastic is 
cheap, recycled plastic struggles to compete. 
iii. Public behaviour is hard to change: source segregation requires consistent 
participation from every household. 
iv. Governance across city departments is notoriously fragmented.

These are not just policy problems; they are engineering design problems:  
• How do you build a segregation system that people actually use?  
• How do you make recycling economically viable without subsidies?  
• How do you site a landfill or WtE plant without community opposition? 
6. Summary 
Solid waste is not just rubbish: it is misplaced resources. The linear economy throws 
those resources away; the circular economy captures them. The 3Rs give a practical 
hierarchy: reduce first, reuse second, recycle third. Engineering methods from 
composting to sanitary landfills handle what remains, and integrated systems bring 
it all together. 
“The journey from linear to circular is not just an economic transition : it is a 
fundamental shift in how we value resources and design our future.” 
“What if every product, instead of ending its life as waste, became the raw 
material for a new product? That simple idea is the foundation of the circular 
economy.”
