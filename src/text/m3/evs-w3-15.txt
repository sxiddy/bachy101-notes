WATER POLLUTION 
1. Definition 
Water pollution is the contamination of water bodies (rivers, lakes, oceans, groundwater) 
by harmful physical, chemical, or biological substances that degrade water quality and 
make it unfit for its intended use. 
1.1 Key Statistics 
i. 2.2 billion people worldwide lack access to safe drinking water (WHO, 2023) 
ii. 1.8 million children die annually from preventable diarrheal diseases caused by 
contaminated water 
iii. 80% of wastewater globally is discharged into waterways without adequate treatment 
iv. Water-related diseases account for 80% of illnesses in developing countries 
 
1.2 WHO Water Quality Standards 
Safe drinking water must meet the following criteria: 
i. pH range: 6.5 to 8.5 
ii. Turbidity: < 1 NTU (Nephelometric Turbidity Unit) 
iii. Total dissolved solids (TDS): < 500 mg/L 
iv. Free from pathogens (E. coli: 0 CFU/100 mL) 
v. Heavy metals below permissible limits (e.g., As < 0.01 mg/L, Pb < 0.01 mg/L) 
 
2. Types of Water Pollution 
2.1 Based on Source 
2.1.1. Surface Water Pollution 
Contamination of rivers, lakes, streams, and oceans — the most visible form of water 
pollution. Examples include foam on the Yamuna River and oil slicks in the ocean.

2.1.2. Groundwater Pollution 
Pesticides, industrial chemicals, and heavy metals seep through soil and contaminate 
aquifers. Particularly dangerous as it affects wells and bore water used for drinking. 
2.1.3. Ocean/Marine Pollution 
Includes oil spills, plastic debris, industrial discharge, and agricultural runoff that reaches 
the sea. The Great Pacific Garbage Patch (twice the size of Texas) is a global example. 
2.1.4. Thermal Pollution 
Hot water discharged from power plants and factories raises the temperature of water 
bodies, reducing dissolved oxygen and harming aquatic life. 
2.2 Based on Pollution Type 
i. Point Source Pollution: Identifiable single source (factory pipe, sewage outlet) 
ii. Non-Point Source Pollution: Diffuse sources like agricultural runoff, urban stormwater 
iii. Transboundary Pollution: Crosses national/state boundaries via river systems 
 
Key Concept: Point sources are easier to regulate because they have a specific, identifiable 
location. Non-point source pollution is harder to control and contributes ~50% of all water 
pollution in many countries. 
 
3. Major Causes of Water Pollution 
3.1 Industrial Sources 
Industries are among the largest contributors to water pollution: 
i. Heavy metals from electroplating, mining (mercury, lead, cadmium, chromium) 
ii. Chemical industries discharge acids, solvents, dyes, and organic compounds 
iii. Textile dyeing effluents contain azo dyes and high BOD (Biochemical Oxygen 
Demand) 
iv. Pharmaceutical industry waste contains active drug compounds resistant to 
biodegradation 
v. Acid Mine Drainage (AMD) — sulfuric acid formed when pyrite in mines oxidizes

3.2 Agricultural Sources 
i. Synthetic fertilizers (nitrates, phosphates) leach into groundwater causing 
eutrophication 
ii. Pesticides and herbicides (DDT, Endosulfan) — persistent organic pollutants (POPs) 
iii. Animal feedlot waste contains pathogens, hormones, antibiotics 
iv. Irrigation return flows carry dissolved salts back to water bodies 
v. Soil erosion from deforested land carries sediment and absorbed chemicals 
 
3.3 Domestic/Municipal Sources 
i. Untreated sewage from urban areas — India generates ~72,368 MLD of sewage; 
treatment capacity: ~31,841 MLD 
ii. Household chemicals — detergents, cleaning agents, medicines flushed down drains 
iii. Solid waste dumping near riverbanks and open drains 
iv. Leachate from unlined landfills contaminating groundwater 
 
3.4 Oil Spills & Marine Pollution 
i. Tanker accidents: Exxon Valdez (1989), 41 million litres; Deepwater Horizon (2010), 
780 million litres 
ii. Routine ship operations: bilge water pumping, illegal discharge at sea 
iii. Offshore drilling platforms — chronic low-level contamination 
 
3.5 Radioactive Waste 
i. Nuclear power plant cooling water may contain trace radioactive isotopes 
ii. Medical and research institution waste — improper disposal 
iii. Uranium and thorium mining — radioactive tailings 
 
India Fact: The Ganga receives about 1.4 billion litres of sewage daily along its course. 
Only about 861 MLD of treatment capacity exists for this volume. NMCG (National 
Mission for Clean Ganga) was launched in 2014 to address this.

4.1.2. Chemical Pollutant Effects 
i. Mercury poisoning (Minamata disease): tremors, neurological damage, birth defects 
ii. Lead (Pb): Cognitive impairment, especially in children under 5 
iii. Nitrates: Methemoglobinemia (Blue Baby Syndrome) — blocks oxygen in infant blood 
iv. Fluoride excess: Dental fluorosis (mottling of teeth) and skeletal fluorosis 
v. Arsenic: Hyperkeratosis, skin cancer, liver and bladder cancer 
 
4.2 Effects on Aquatic Ecosystems 
4.2.1. Eutrophication 
Excess nitrogen and phosphorus from fertilizers trigger explosive algal blooms. When 
algae die, decomposing bacteria consume oxygen, creating hypoxic 'dead zones' where fish 
and aquatic life cannot survive. The Gulf of Mexico dead zone (~22,720 km²) is a m ajor 
example.

4.2.2. Biomagnification 
Persistent pollutants like DDT and mercury accumulate in organisms and become 
increasingly concentrated as they move up the food chain. A fish may contain 10× the 
mercury of its prey; a bird eating that fish may contain 100× the baseline level. 
4.2.3. Biodiversity Loss 
i. Sensitive species (mayflies, trout, salmon) disappear first, indicating pollution levels 
ii. Loss of keystone species disrupts entire food webs 
iii. Coral bleaching occurs when ocean temperatures rise or pollutants are absorbed 
iv. Oil spills smother invertebrates, fish eggs, and shore birds 
4.2.4. Habitat Destruction 
i. Sedimentation from runoff smothers fish spawning grounds 
ii. Plastic debris entangles and kills marine mammals and seabirds 
iii. Wetlands degraded by agricultural chemicals lose their filtration and biodiversity 
functions 
 
5. Control & Prevention of Water Pollution 
5.1 Sewage Treatment: Three-Stage Process 
Wastewater treatment is the most important engineering intervention: 
Stage 1: Primary Treatment (Physical) 
 
i. Screening: Bars and screens remove large debris (rags, sticks, plastics) 
ii. Grit Chamber: Removes sand, grit, and heavy particles 
iii. Sedimentation Tank (Primary Clarifier):  Allows suspended solids to settle as 
'primary sludge' 
iv. Outcome: Removes ~60% of suspended solids, ~30% of BOD

Stage 2: Secondary Treatment (Biological) 
i. Activated Sludge Process: Microorganisms consume dissolved organic matter 
ii. Trickling Filters: Wastewater trickles over a bed of rocks/media coated with biofilm 
iii. Rotating Biological Contactors (RBCs): Spinning discs with microbial biofilm 
iv. Outcome: Removes 85–95% of BOD and most suspended solids 
 
Stage 3: Tertiary Treatment (Advanced) 
i. Nutrient Removal: Biological/chemical removal of nitrogen and phosphorus 
ii. UV Disinfection / Chlorination: Kills remaining pathogens 
iii. Reverse Osmosis / Membrane filtration: Removes dissolved chemicals 
iv. Outcome: Produces near-drinking quality effluent 
 
In India, the Sewage Treatment Plant (STP) capacity needs to be tripled to treat all 
generated sewage. As of 2022, only 28% of sewage generated in Class -I cities and Class-
II towns is treated. 
 
5.2 Industrial Pollution Control 
i. Zero Liquid Discharge (ZLD): Industries recycle all wastewater — no discharge to 
environment 
ii. Effluent Treatment Plants (ETPs): Mandatory before discharge 
iii. Common Effluent Treatment Plants (CETPs): Shared infrastructure for small industries 
iv. Ion Exchange / Activated Carbon: Remove heavy metals and organic compounds 
v. Regular monitoring by Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) 
 
5.3 Agricultural Practices 
i. Integrated Pest Management (IPM): Minimize chemical pesticide use 
ii. Precision farming: Apply fertilizers only where and when needed 
iii. Buffer strips: Vegetation strips along waterways to filter runoff 
iv. Constructed wetlands: Engineered systems using plants to treat agricultural runoff 
v. Organic farming: Eliminates synthetic pesticide and fertilizer contamination

5.4 Nature-Based Solutions 
i. Phytoremediation: Plants like water hyacinth and vetiver grass absorb pollutants 
ii. Bioremediation: Microorganisms break down organic pollutants 
iii. Riparian buffers and wetland restoration reduce non-point source pollution 
iv. Rainwater harvesting reduces storm runoff and groundwater depletion 
 
5.5 Legislation & Policy (India) 
i. Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 — established PCBs 
ii. Environment Protection Act, 1986 — umbrella legislation 
iii. National Mission for Clean Ganga (NMCG) / Namami Gange Programme (2015) 
iv. Coastal Regulation Zone (CRZ) Notification — protects coastal waters 
v. Bureau of Indian Standards IS 10500: 2012 — Drinking water quality standards 
 
6. Case Studies 
6.1 Minamata Disease, Japan (1956) 
Chisso Chemical Company discharged methylmercury into Minamata Bay. The mercury 
bioaccumulated in fish and shellfish, the local staple food. Over 2,000 people died and 
thousands more suffered neurological damage. This tragedy led to the Minamata 
Convention on Mercury (2013), a global treaty. 
 
6.2 Yamuna River, India 
The Yamuna receives 58% of Delhi's total waste. Near Okhla, a thick white foam forms 
due to phosphate-laden detergents. Over 22 drains discharge directly into the river. Despite 
multiple Yamuna Action Plans, the river remains critically polluted with BOD l evels 
exceeding standards by 50× in some stretches.

6.3 Deepwater Horizon Oil Spill (2010) 
An explosion on the Deepwater Horizon rig released approximately 4.9 million barrels 
(780 million litres) of crude oil into the Gulf of Mexico over 87 days. Over 400 species 
were affected, including sea turtles, dolphins, and brown pelicans. Cleanup costs exceeded 
$65 billion. 
 
7. Summary & Key Takeaways 
 
Water is the most essential resource for life. Protecting it from pollution is both an 
environmental and humanitarian imperative. 
 
i. Water pollution affects 2.2 billion people and kills millions annually through 
waterborne diseases 
ii. Industrial, agricultural, and domestic waste are the three primary pollutants 
iii. Effects range from diarrheal diseases to ecosystem collapse through eutrophication 
iv. Sewage treatment (primary, secondary, tertiary) is the most effective control technology 
v. Legislation (Water Act 1974, NMCG) + citizen awareness are essential for sustainable 
solutions
