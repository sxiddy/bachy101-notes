Global Warming 
1. Introduction 
Of all the environmental challenges facing humanity in the twenty -first century, 
global warming stands out as the most far -reaching and consequential. It is not a 
distant or hypothetical threat — it is a measurable, ongoing transformation of the 
Earth's climate system, driven overwhelmingly by human activity and already 
reshaping ecosystems, weather patterns, sea levels, and human societies across every 
continent. 
Global warming refers to the long-term rise in the average surface temperature of 
the Earth, primarily due to the accumulation of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere 
as a result of industrial, agricultural, and land-use activities. The Intergovernmental 
Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) — the foremost scientific body assessing climate 
science — has confirmed that the Earth's average surface temperature has risen by 
approximately 1.1 degrees Celsius above pre -industrial levels (the period before 
1850) and is on course to exceed 1.5 degrees Celsius within the coming decades if 
current emission trends continue. 
For engineering students, this topic is not merely academic. Every major engineering 
domain — civil, mechanical, chemical, electrical, and environmental — is 
implicated both in causing global warming and in the solutions required to address 
it. Understanding the science, causes, and consequences of global warming is a 
foundational professional competency for the engineers who will design the 
infrastructure and technologies of the future. 
2. The Greenhouse Effect 
2.1 The Natural Greenhouse Effect  
To understand global warming, one must first understand the greenhouse effect — 
a natural and essential atmospheric process without which life on Earth as we know 
it would not exist. The Sun emits energy primarily as short -wave radiation, which 
passes relatively easily through the Earth's atmosphere and warms the planet's 
surface. The warmed surface then re -emits this energy as long -wave infrared 
radiation (heat) back towards space. 
Certain gases in the atmosph ere — known as greenhouse gases — absorb this 
outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it in all directions, including back towards 
the Earth's surface. This trapping of heat keeps the planet at an average temperature 
of approximately 15 degrees Celsius. Wi thout the natural greenhouse effect, the

2.2 The Enhanced Greenhouse Effect 
The problem arises when human activities significantly increase the concentration 
of greenhouse gases in the atmosphere beyond natural levels. This enhanced 
greenhouse effect traps additional heat, raising global temperatures beyon d what 
natural variability can account for. It is this human -amplified version of the 
greenhouse effect that drives contemporary global warming. 
The primary greenhouse gases and their sources are as follows: 
Carbon Dioxide (CO2): The most significant contributor to anthropogenic (human-
caused) warming. It is released through the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil, 
and natural gas for energy and transportation, as well as through deforestation and 
cement production. CO2 persists in the atmosphere for hundreds to thousands of 
years, making it an extremely long-term driver of warming. 
Methane (CH4): A potent greenhouse gas approximately 80 times more powerful 
than CO2 over a 20 -year period. It is emitted from livestock digestion (enteric 
fermentation), rice paddies, landfills, and the extraction and transport of fossil fuels, 
particularly natural gas. 
Nitrous Oxide (N2O): Released primarily from agricultural activities, especially 
the use of synthetic nitrogen fertilisers and the management of animal manure. It is 
approximately 265 times more potent than CO2 over a 100-year period. 
Water Vapour: The most abundant greenhouse gas by volume, but it acts primarily 
as a feedback amplifier rather than a direct driver of warming. As temperatures rise, 
more water evaporates, which traps more heat, which causes further warming — a 
positive feedback loop. 
Fluorinated Gases: Synthetic gases used in refrigeration, air conditioning, and 
manufacturing processes. Though present in smaller quantities, they are thousands 
of times more potent than CO2 and can persist in the atmosphere for centuries. 
The concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere has risen from approximately 280 parts 
per million (ppm) during pre-industrial times to over 420 ppm today — the highest 
level in at least the past 800,000 years as determined by ice core records.

3. Causes of Global Warming  
3.1 Combustion of Fossil Fuels  
The single largest source of greenhouse gas emissions globally is the burning of 
fossil fuels for energy — electricity generation, heating, and transportation. Coal-
fired power plants, gasoline and diesel vehicles, and industrial furnaces collectively 
release billions of tonnes of CO2 into the atmosphere every year. The Industrial 
Revolution, which began in the late eighteenth century in Britain and spread globally 
through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, marked the beginning of large-scale 
fossil fuel combustion. Since then, cumulative emissions have altered the 
composition of the atmosphere in a geologically unprecedented manner. 
3.2 Deforestation and Land Use Change  
Forests are among the Earth's most important carbon sinks — they absorb CO2 from 
the atmosphere through photosynthesis and store it in biomass and soil. When forests 
are cleared for agriculture, urban development, or t imber, this stored carbon is 
released back into the atmosphere, either immediately through burning or gradually 
through decomposition. Tropical deforestation, particularly in the Amazon basin, the 
Congo rainforest, and Southeast Asian forests, contributes significantly to annual 
global greenhouse gas emissions. Additionally, the conversion of forests to 
agricultural land reduces the planet's capacity to absorb future emissions. 
3.3 Industrial Processes and Manufacturing 
Beyond energy combustion, various industrial processes directly emit greenhouse 
gases as by -products of chemical reactions. Cement production, for example, 
releases CO2 as limestone is heated to produce lime. Steel manufacturing, 
aluminium smelting, and chemical production all contribute substantial emissions. 
The global construction and manufacturing boom driven by urbanisation and 
population growth — especially in developing economies — has significantly 
expanded this category of emissions in recent decades. 
3.4 Agriculture and Livestock 
The agricultural sector contributes to global warming through multiple pathways. 
Livestock — particularly cattle — produce methane through enteric fermentation, 
the digestive process in their stomachs. Rice paddies emit methane as organic matter 
decomposes in flooded, anaerobic conditions. The application of nitrogen fertilisers 
releases nitrous oxide. Together, agriculture accounts for approximately 10 to 12 
percent of global greenhouse gas emissions, a share that is growing as global food 
demand increases with population growth.

3.5 Waste Management  
Landfills are a significant and often underappreciated source of methane emissions. 
When organic waste — food scraps, paper, garden waste — decomposes in the 
absence of oxygen in a landfill, it produces metha ne, which escapes into the 
atmosphere. Wastewater treatment processes also release both methane and nitrous 
oxide. As urbanisation increases and waste generation grows, this sector's 
contribution to total emissions is rising. 
4. Consequences of Global Warm ing 
4.1 Rising Temperatures and Heatwaves  
The most direct consequence of global warming is the increase in average surface 
temperatures. This manifests not just as gradual background warming but as a 
dramatic increase in the frequency, intensity, and durat ion of heatwaves. Extreme 
heat events that once occurred once every fifty years are now occurring multiple 
times per decade in many regions. In India, deadly heat waves have intensified 
significantly, with temperatures exceeding 45 to 48 degrees Celsius in  parts of 
Rajasthan, Odisha, and Andhra Pradesh with increasing regularity, posing acute 
risks to outdoor workers, the elderly, and urban populations. 
4.2 Melting Ice and Rising Sea Levels  
Warmer temperatures are accelerating the melting of glaciers, ice caps, and the polar 
ice sheets of Greenland and Antarctica. This melting, combined with the thermal 
expansion of seawater as it warms, is causing global sea levels to rise. Sea levels 
have risen by approximately 20 centimetres since 1900, and the rate of ri se is 
accelerating. The IPCC projects that sea levels could rise by between 0.3 and 1 metre 
by the end of this century, with higher scenarios possible if the West Antarctic Ice 
Sheet destabilises. Low-lying coastal cities — including Mumbai, Kolkata, Chennai, 
and Kochi in India — face increasing risks of flooding and permanent inundation. 
Small island nations such as the Maldives and Tuvalu face existential threats to their 
very existence. 
4.3 Altered Precipitation Patterns and Extreme Weather 
Global warming disrupts the hydrological cycle — the movement of water through 
evaporation, condensation, and precipitation. Warmer air holds more moisture, 
which intensifies rainfall events when precipitation does occur, leading to more

severe floods. At the same time, increased evaporation from soil and vegetation dries 
out regions that are already prone to drought, making droughts longer and more 
severe. The Indian monsoon system is increasingly erratic — periods of intense 
flooding in some years are followed by defi cient monsoons in others — with 
significant implications for agricultural productivity, water security, and disaster 
management. 
4.4 Ocean Acidification and Marine Ecosystem Disruption 
The oceans absorb approximately one -quarter of all CO2 emitted by human  
activities. While this moderates the pace of atmospheric warming, it has a severe 
side effect: when CO2 dissolves in seawater, it forms carbonic acid, lowering the pH 
of the ocean — a process known as ocean acidification. Since the industrial era 
began, the ocean's pH has dropped by approximately 0.1 units, representing a 26 
percent increase in acidity. This affects the ability of shellfish, corals, and other 
marine organisms to build calcium carbonate shells and skeletons. Coral reefs — 
which support roughly 25 percent of all marine species — are experiencing mass 
bleaching events with increasing frequency as ocean temperatures rise above critical 
thresholds. 
4.5 Impacts on Biodiversity and Ecosystems 
Climate zones are shifting poleward and to higher altit udes as temperatures rise, 
forcing species to migrate to stay within their thermal comfort zones. Species that 
cannot migrate quickly enough face local extinction. The timing of seasonal 
biological events — such as flowering, migration, and breeding — is s hifting, 
disrupting the ecological relationships between species that have co -evolved over 
millennia. The IPCC has projected that at 1.5 degrees Celsius of warming, a 
significant fraction of species face increased extinction risk; at 2 degrees Celsius, 
those risks grow substantially. The Himalayan and Western Ghats ecosystems, both 
biodiversity hotspots of global significance, are considered highly vulnerable. 
4.6 Human Health and Food Security 
Global warming poses direct and indirect threats to human healt h. Direct threats 
include increased mortality from heatwaves, greater exposure to vector -borne 
diseases such as malaria and dengue fever as disease -carrying mosquitoes expand 
their geographic range, and respiratory diseases worsened by air pollution and 
wildfire smoke. Indirect threats operate through disruptions to food and water 
security. Rising temperatures, changing rainfall patterns, and more frequent extreme 
events reduce agricultural yields and increase crop failures, threatening food security 
for vulnerable populations. The World Health Organisation projects that global

5. Mitigation Strategies  
Mitigation refers to actions that reduce or prevent the emission of greenhouse gases, 
thereby limiting the extent of future warming. Effective mitigation requires 
coordinated action across energy, transport, industry, land use, and consumption 
patterns. 
5.1 Transition to Renewable Energy 
Replacing coal, oil, and gas with solar, wind, hydroelectric, and geothermal energy 
sources is the most impactful single mitigation action available. The cost of solar 
photovoltaic and wind power has fallen by over 80 percent in the past decade, 
making renewables economically competitive with fossil fuels in most parts of the 
world. India, with its exceptional solar resource, has made significant strides through 
the National Solar Mission and aims to achieve 500 GW of non -fossil fuel energy 
capacity by 2030. 
5.2 Energy Efficiency 
Reducing the amount of energy required to deliver the same level of service — 
through better insulation in buildings, more efficient appliances and industrial 
processes, and improved vehicle fuel economy — is a cost-effective complement to 
the clean energy transition. Energy efficiency improvements can reduce global 
energy demand substantially, easing the scale and pace of the renewable energy 
build-out required. 
5.3 Reforestation and Carbon Sequestration  
Restoring degraded forests and planting new trees enhances the natural carbon sink 
capacity of the land. India's National Action Plan on Climate Change includes the 
Green India Mission, which aims to increase forest and tree cover and improve the 
quality of existing forests. Carbon capture and storage (CCS) technologies, which 
capture CO2 from industrial sources and store it underground, represent an emerging 
technological approach to reducing atmospheric concentrations. 
5.4 Sustainable Agriculture and Waste Management 
Reducing methane and nitrous oxide emissions from agriculture through improved 
livestock management, the use of precision fertilisation techniques, and the adoption 
of sustainable rice cultivation methods can significantly reduce the agricultur al 
sector's climate footprint. Improving waste management through composting, waste-

5.5 International Climate Agreements 
The Paris Agreement of 2015 is the central international framework for coordinating 
global climate action. Under the Agreement, countries submitted nationally 
determined contributions — pledges to reduce emissions and transition to cleaner 
economies. The Agreement's central goal is to limit global warming to well below 2 
degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels and to pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 
degrees Celsius. India's nationally determined contribution includes achieving net 
zero emissions by 2070 and having 50 percent of its installe d electricity capacity 
from non-fossil fuel sources by 2030. 
6. Adaptation Strategies 
While mitigation aims to prevent further warming, adaptation refers to adjustments 
in human systems and natural processes in response to actual or anticipated warming 
and its effects. Even if all emissions were stopped today, the warming already locked 
into the climate system due to accumulated greenhouse gases would continue for 
decades. Adaptation is therefore not a substitute for mitigation but an essential 
complement to it. 
Adaptation strategies include developing drought -resistant and heat-tolerant crop 
varieties to maintain agricultural productivity under changed conditions. Building 
flood defences, sea walls, and storm surge barriers protects coastal and low -lying 
communities. Urban planning that incorporates green spaces, reflective surfaces, and 
efficient cooling reduces the urban heat island effect and protects city populations 
during heatwaves. Integrated water resource management — including rainwater 
harvesting, groundwater recharge, and efficient irrigation — builds resilience to 
variable precipitation patterns. 
For India, adaptation is particularly urgent. The country is home to over 1.4 billion 
people, a large proportion of whom are directly dependent on rain -fed agriculture 
and are highly exposed to climate -related hazards including floods, droughts, 
cyclones, and heatwaves. The National Action Plan on Climate Change, launched in 
2008, laid out eight national missions covering solar energy, energy efficiency, 
water, the Himalayan ecosystem, the Green India initiative, sustainable agriculture, 
strategic knowledge for climate change, and sustainable habitats.

7. The Role of Engineers in Addressing Global Warming 
Engineers occupy a uniquely powerful position in t he global response to climate 
change. The technologies and infrastructure that currently drive emissions — coal 
power plants, internal combustion engines, energy-intensive industrial processes — 
were designed and built by engineers. The solutions that will  replace them — 
renewable energy systems, electric vehicles, green buildings, carbon-neutral cement, 
smart grids, and climate-resilient infrastructure — will also be designed and built by 
engineers. 
As a first -year engineering student, you may feel distant  from these large-scale 
challenges. But the choices you make in your professional career — about materials, 
energy sources, design standards, operational efficiency, and the systems you help 
build — will collectively determine the trajectory of humanity's response to global 
warming. The engineering profession has a responsibility not just to clients and 
employers, but to the broader public and to future generations who will inherit the 
consequences of the decisions made today. 
Integrating environmental thinking into engineering practice is no longer optional. 
It is a core professional competency demanded by employers, regulators, and 
society. Whether you work in construction, manufacturing, energy, water, 
transportation, or information technology, the impera tive to design and operate 
systems that are compatible with a stable climate will shape your career from 
beginning to end. 
8. Conclusion 
Global warming is the defining environmental challenge of our era. It is rooted in 
the enhanced greenhouse effect drive n by the accumulation of carbon dioxide, 
methane, nitrous oxide, and other gases released through fossil fuel combustion, 
deforestation, agriculture, and industrial processes. Its consequences — rising 
temperatures, melting ice, sea level rise, extreme wea ther, ocean acidification, 
biodiversity loss, and threats to human health and food security — are already being 
felt and will intensify unless aggressive action is taken. 
Addressing global warming requires both mitigation — reducing greenhouse gas 
emissions through clean energy, efficiency, forest protection, and sustainable land 
use — and adaptation — adjusting infrastructure, agriculture, and communities to 
the warming that has already been set in motion. International agreements like the 
Paris Agreement provide a framework, but the actual work of transformation will be 
carried out through technology, policy, investment, and individual and collective 
behaviour change at every level of society.

Key Takeaways  
• Global warming is the long-term rise in Earth's average surface temperature 
due to the enhanced greenhouse effect caused by human activities. 
• The major greenhouse gases are CO2, methane, nitrous oxide, and fluorinated 
gases, with fossil fuel combustion being the largest source. 
• Consequences include heatwaves, sea level rise, extreme weather, ocean 
acidification, biodiversity loss, and threats to food and human health. 
• Mitigation involves reducing emissions through renewable energy, efficiency, 
reforestation, and sustainable practices. 
• Adaptation involves adjusting systems to cope with unavoidable warming 
through climate-resilient infrastructure, agriculture, and water management. 
• Engineers have a central role in both causing and solving the climate crisis; 
environmental competence is a core professional responsibility.
