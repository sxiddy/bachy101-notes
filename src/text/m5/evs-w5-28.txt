Climate Change Science 
1. Introduction 
Climate change science is arguably the most consequential topic in environmental 
science, with direct implications for the careers of every future engineer. This lecture 
examines what climate change is scientifically, what is causing it, what evidence 
supports it, what its consequences are, and what students and engineers can do to 
address it. 
2. The Greenhouse Effect 
The greenhouse effect is a natural and essential process — without it, Earth would 
be frozen. Sunlight passes through the atmosphere and warms the surface. The 
surface then radiates heat back upward, and greenhouse gases in the atmosphere 
absorb this outgoing heat and redirect some of it back down. This is why Earth is 
warm enough to support life. Without the greenhouse effect, Earth's average 
temperature would be approximately minus 18 degrees Celsius — a frozen 
wasteland. 
2.1 Natural versus Enhanced Greenhouse Effect 
● Natural greenhouse effect = beneficial and necessary for life. 
● Enhanced greenhouse effect (caused by human emissions) = the actual problem. 
An analogy: a thin blanket keeps a person warm, but piling five blankets on top of 
one another causes overheating. The same principle applies to the atmosphere , the 
natural blanket of greenhouse gases is necessary, but human emissions are adding 
extra layers that trap excess heat. 
2.2 Key Greenhouse Gases 
The greenhouse gases of greatest concern are carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane, nitrous 
oxide, and water vapour. CO₂ is the headline gas, the primary gas being added to the 
atmosphere through the burning of coal, oil, and gas. Methane is shorter-lived in the 
atmosphere but has a far greater warming power, roughly 84 times that of CO₂ over 
a 20-year period.

3. Evidence for Climate Change 
The case for human -induced climate change rests on four independent pillars of 
evidence, all pointing to the same conclusion. 
3.1 Rising Temperatures 
Global average surface temperatures have increased by approximately 1.1°C above 
pre-industrial levels. Nineteen of the twenty hottest years on record have occurred 
since the year 2000. This trend is independently confirmed by NASA, NOAA, the 
UK Met Office, and the Japan Meteorological Agency, each maintaining separate 
datasets that show the same pattern. 
3.2 Rising CO₂ Concentration 
Before industrialisation, atmospheric CO₂ concentration was approximately 280 
parts per million (ppm). Today it exceeds 420 ppm, a level not seen on Earth for at 
least 3 million years. This is confirmed through ice cores drilled in Antarctica, which 
trap ancient air bubbles going back 800,000 years, providing a direct measurement 
of past atmospheric composition. 
3.3 Melting Ice and Sea Level Rise 
Arctic sea ice area has declined by roughly 13% per decade since satellite records 
began in 1979. The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets are losing mass, and global 
mean sea level has risen approximately 20 centimetres since 1900, with the rate of 
rise accelerating in recent decades. 
3.4 Ocean Warming and Acidification 
The oceans have absorb ed approximately 90% of the excess heat trapped by the 
enhanced greenhouse effect and about 25 –30% of human CO₂ emissions. This 
absorbed CO₂ reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, measurably lowering 
ocean pH and threatening shellfish, corals, and the wider marine food web. 
These four independent lines of evidence , from completely separate measurement 
systems, converge on the same conclusion, which is what gives scientists such high 
confidence in human-induced climate change.

4. Causes of Climate Change 
The primary driver of current climate change is human activity. 
4.1 Human (Anthropogenic) Causes 
● Fossil fuel combustion, the single biggest source, from burning coal, oil, and gas 
for electricity, industry, and transport, accounting for about 75% of global 
greenhouse gas emissions. 
● Deforestation, forests are carbon sinks that absorb CO₂; cutting or burning them 
releases stored carbon and reduces sink capacity, accounting for roughly 10–15% 
of global emissions. 
● Agriculture, livestock produce methane through digestion, flooded rice paddies 
emit methane, and nitrogen fertilisers release nitrous oxide. 
● Industrial processes , cement and steel manufacturing add significant CO₂; 
cement production alone accounts for about 8% of global emissions. 
4.2 Natural Factors and Why They Do Not Explain Current Warming 
Volcanic eruptions, changes in solar output, and Earth's orbital cycles (the 
Milankovitch cycles) have all shaped climate over geological timescales. However, 
solar output has actually slightly decreased since the 1980s while temperatures have 
continued to rise, and volcanic eruptions cause only short -term cooling, not long -
term wa rming. Natural factors alone cannot explain the rate and pattern of the 
warming currently being observed, the signal is unambiguously human in origin. 
5. Consequences of Climate Change 
Climate change is not a future problem, many of its consequences are already visible 
today, and they compound over time. 
 
5.1 Weather Extremes 
A warmer atmosphere holds more moisture, about 7% more per degree of warming, 
which intensifies the water cycle, producing heavier rainfall and flooding in some

regions and longer, more severe droughts in others. Heatwaves are becoming more 
frequent and more deadly, and tropical cyclones are expected to become more 
intense with higher rainfall rates. For India, a key concern is disruption to the 
monsoon system, on which over a billion p eople depend for agriculture and 
freshwater. 
5.2 Sea Level Rise 
Sea level rise is a slow-moving but massive risk. Coastal cities such as Mumbai and 
Chennai, and the entire Bengal delta region, face increasing threats from storm 
surges and inundation. Small island nations in the Pacific are already experiencing 
existential threats from this phenomenon. 
5.3 Ecosystem Stress 
Ecosystems are under significant stress. Coral reefs, which support roughly 25% of 
all marine species, begin bleaching at just 1°C above normal summer temperatures. 
At 2°C of warming, over 99% of tropical reefs are projected to be severely degraded. 
5.4 Food Security 
Climate change is already reducing yields of key crops such as wheat, rice, and 
maize in tropical regions. Combined with popu lation growth, this creates serious 
risks of food insecurity, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia. 
5.5 Health Impacts 
Direct health impacts include increased heat-related illness, the expansion of vector-
borne diseases such as malaria and den gue into new regions, and worsening air 
quality. 
5.6 Regional Risks for India and South Asia 
The Indian subcontinent faces serious region -specific threats from heatwaves, 
monsoon disruption, glacial melt in the Himalayas, and sea level rise in coastal cities. 
6. Mitigation, Adaptation, and Policy

Responding to climate change requires two complementary strategies: mitigation, 
which reduces the emissions causing the problem, and adaptation, which involves 
designing systems that can cope with changes already underway. 
6.1 Mitigation 
The energy transition is the centrepiece of mitigation , replacing fossil fuel power 
with solar, wind, hydro, and other renewables. India is already pursuing this at scale, 
with some of the world's largest solar installations and an ambitious renewable 
energy target. Energy efficiency also matters enormously: well-designed buildings, 
efficient motors, and optimised transport systems all reduce demand and emissions. 
Carbon capture technologies, which physically remove CO₂ from the atmosphere or 
capture it at industrial sources, form a growing field that may be essential for hard-
to-decarbonise industries such as cement and steel. 
6.2 Adaptation 
Adaptation is fundamentally an engineering exercise: flood defences, climate -
resilient road and bridge design, drought -resistant water supply systems, and heat -
resilient urban planning, including green roofs, urban trees, and cool pavements. 
Every infrastructure project going forward needs to be designed with climate risk in 
mind, and building co des, drainage standards, and coastal zone planning are being 
revised globally to account for a changing climate. 
6.3 Policy Frameworks 
Internationally, the Paris Agreement commits nations to limiting warming to well 
below 2°C, ideally 1.5°C. India has comm itted to achieving net -zero emissions by 
2070 and is aggressively ramping up renewable energy deployment. The IPCC 
synthesises the underlying science and informs global climate policy. 
 
7. Summary and Key Takeaways 
i. The greenhouse effect is natural and necessary; the problem is its enhancement 
by human emissions.

ii. The evidence for human-caused climate change is overwhelming and comes from 
multiple independent sources: temperature records, CO₂ data, ice cores, and 
ocean measurements. 
iii. Fossil fuels, deforestatio n, and agriculture are the primary human drivers of 
climate change. 
iv. The consequences are already unfolding, including extreme weather, sea level 
rise, ecosystem stress, and food and health risks. 
v. Solutions exist , renewable energy, efficiency, and adaptatio n design , and 
engineers are central to delivering them. 
vi. International frameworks such as the Paris Agreement provide policy 
scaffolding, but the technical solutions must come from engineering and related 
fields. 
Climate change is not a distant threat; it is a scientific reality shaping the world 
today. Understanding the underlying science empowers informed decision -making 
and the development of sustainable solutions. As future engineers and professionals, 
this knowledge forms the foundation for building a more resilient tomorrow.
