Earth as a Life Support System 
Earth functions as a life support system by providing the essential resources and 
conditions needed by all living organisms. These include: 
Essential Resources: 
 Air to breathe → Atmosphere 
 Water to drink → Hydrosphere 
 Food sources → Biosphere 
 Solid ground and nutrients → Lithosphere 
These four components interact to maintain a stable and liveable environment. 
What is a “Sphere”? 
The word sphere means “to surround or encompass.” Earth is made  up of four 
major spheres that support life: 
           Sphere             Root Word→Meaning 
 Lithosphere  litho→refers to rocks and minerals 
 Hydrosphere hydro→refers to water 
 Biosphere           bio→refers to life 
 Atmosphere atmo→refers to steam and vapor 
All four spheres work together to sustain life: The lithosphere provides land and 
minerals. The hydrosphere supplies water.  The atmosphere provides air and 
weather. The biosphere includes all living organisms, which depend on the 
other three spheres to survive. 
1. Atmosphere: Atmosphere, it is the gaseous layer that surrounds Earth’s 
surface and is crucial for life. It is made up of gases and tiny water particles that 
form a thin protective envelope. The atmosphere plays a vital role in providing 
oxygen for breathing, protecti ng living organisms from harmful solar radiation, 
and helping regulate the planet’s temperature. It also supports weather systems 
and the water cycle. The air in the atmosphere is composed mainly of nitrogen 
and oxygen. Nitrogen makes up 78% of the total v olume, while oxygen 
accounts for 21%. The remaining 1% includes other gases such as water vapor,

1.1 Troposphere:  The troposphere is the lowest layer  of Earth's atmosphere , 
directly above the surface. It is the layer where all life exists, including humans, 
animals, and plants. It is also where birds fly and where airplanes operate. The 
troposphere plays a critical role in sustaining life by holding mo st of the 
atmosphere's mass and almost all the  weather phenomena, such as rain, snow, 
storms, clouds, and wind. This layer is warmest near the Earth's surface  due to 
heat radiating from the ground. However, as altitude increases, the temperature 
decreases steadily, making higher elevations colder. The troposphere extends 
from 0 to about 15 kilometres (km) above the Earth's surface, although this can 
vary slightly with latitude and weather conditions. At  the very top of the 
troposphere is a boundary called t he tropopause. This acts like a lid, preventing 
weather systems from rising into the layers above, such as the stratosphere. The 
temperature in the tropopause becomes stable before starting to rise again in the 
higher atmospheric layers. The presence of th e tropopause helps to contain 
weather within the troposphere and contributes to the climate regulation  on 
Earth. 
1.2 Stratosphere: The stratosphere is the second layer of Earth’s atmosphere, 
positioned directly above the troposphere. It begins around 15 kilometres above 
the Earth's surface and extends up to approximately 50 kilometres in altitude. 
One of the most important features of the stratosphere is the presence of  a high 
concentration of ozone gas, forming the ozone layer. The ozone layer plays a 
crucial role in protecting life on Earth by absorbing the majority of the Sun’s 
harmful ultraviolet (UV) radiation. Without this protective shield, life as we 
know it coul d not exist. Unlike the troposphere, the temperature in the 
stratosphere increases with altitude, due to the absorption of solar energy by

ozone molecules. Because the air in the stratosphere is very stable and dry, it is 
ideal for high-altitude aircraft a nd weather balloons , which often operate in 
this layer. The boundary between the stratosphere and the mesosphere above it 
is known as the stratopause. 
 
1.3 Mesosphere:  The mesosphere is the third layer  of Earth’s atmosphere, 
located above the stratosphere  and below the thermosphere. It begins at an 
altitude of around 50 kilometres and extends up to about 85 kilometres above 
the Earth’s surface. This layer is known as the coldest layer of the atmosphere, 
with temperatures that can drop as low as –90°C. In the mesosphere, water 
vapor often freezes, forming ice clouds. These clouds, sometimes called 
noctilucent clouds , are visible in the twilight sky and are among the highest 
clouds in Earth's atmosphere. The air pressure is extremely low, and the density 
of air molecules is very thin, making this layer difficult to study.  The 
mesopause marks the boundary between the mesosphere and the thermosphere 
above it. This region is also where meteors burn up  upon entering Earth’s 
atmosphere due to friction with the air, creating what we see as shooting stars. 
1.4 Thermosphere: Thermosphere lies above the mesosphere , and this is a 
region where the temperature increases as you go higher up. The temperature 
increase is caused due to the absorption of energetic ultraviol et and X -ray 
radiation from the sun. Satellites orbit Earth within the thermosphere. 
Temperatures in the upper thermosphere can range from about 500° to 2,000° C

1.5 Exosphere: Exosphere is the final frontier of the Earth’s gaseous envelope. 
The air in the exosphere is constantly but gradually leaking out of the Earth’s 
atmosphere into outer space. There is no clear-cut upper boundary where the 
exosphere finally fades away into space.

2. Hydrosphere: The hydrosphere refers to the total amount of water  found 
on Earth’s surface, underground, and in the atmosphere. This includes liquid 
water, ice, and water vapor. On Earth, most of the water (around 97%) exists in 
the oceans, while about 2% is found in lakes and rivers. However, only 1% of 
Earth’s water i s available as freshwater for human use, found in rivers, lakes, 
streams, and underground sources.  The hydrosphere plays a critical role in 
sustaining life , as water is essential for the survival of all organisms. It also 
helps regulate the atmosphere and climate by distributing heat around the 
planet. Water moves through the hydrosphere in a continuous cycle, beginning 
with evaporation into the air, followed by condensation into clouds, and finally 
returning to the surface as rain or snow. This precipitati on collects in rivers, 
lakes, and oceans, completing the cycle.  The hydrosphere includes not just 
saltwater and freshwater, but also frozen water (such as glaciers and ice caps), 
groundwater, and water in the lower layers of the atmosphere. Its interaction  
with other spheres, like the atmosphere and biosphere, is vital for maintaining 
ecosystem balance and supporting life on Earth. 
 
The Water Cycle (Hydrologic Cycle) 
The water cycle is the continuous movement of water through the Earth’s 
hydrosphere. It starts with evaporation, where water from oceans, lakes, and 
rivers turns into vapor due to the Sun’s heat. This vapor cools and changes into 
droplets through condensation, forming clouds.  When the droplets grow heavy, 
they fall as precipitation (rain, snow, etc.). The water then flows as runoff into

3. Geosphere 
The geosphere refers to the solid portions  of the Earth, extending from the 
inner core to the crust. It includes rocks, minerals, soil, mountains, volcanoes, 
and all landforms that shape the Earth's surface and interior. The geosphere 
plays a vital role in Earth's overall system by providing the foundation for 
terrestrial life and supporting natural processes such as plate tectonics, mountain 
formation, and erosion . It interacts closely with other spheres: with the 
atmosphere, volcanic eruptions release gases and ash that influence weather and 
climate; with the hydrosphere, water shapes the landscape through erosion and 
sediment deposition; and with the biosphere, s oil formed from rock and organic 
matter supports plant life, which in turn stabilizes land  and affects rock 
breakdown. These continuous interactions help regulate Earth's systems and 
drive the natural cycles essential for life.

3.1. Major Components of the Geosphere: 
3.1.1. Earth’s Interior layers: 
a. Core: 
Inner Core: It refers to a solid part mainly composed of iron and nickel. 
Outer Core: It is a liquid, responsible for Earth’s magnetic field. 
b. Mantle:  
Semi-solid rock layer beneath the crust. It is  involved in convection currents 
that drive plate tectonics. 
c. Crust: 
The crust is known as the Earth’s outermost layer. It contains continental and 
oceanic plates. 
3.1.2. Rocks and Minerals: 
Rocks: Rocks are naturally occurring solid aggregates of one or  more minerals 
or mineraloids. They are classified into three major types based on their origin 
and formation processes, including igneous rocks, sedimentary rocks, and 
metamorphic rocks.

Minerals: Minerals are naturally occurring, inorganic solids that have a definite 
chemical composition and a crystalline structure. 
3.1.3. Soil: 
Soil is the thin layer of material covering the Earth’s surface, formed through 
the weathering of rocks and the decay of organic matter (like dead plants and 
animals). It contains minerals, organic matter, water, and air. Supports plant life 
and plays a role in nutrient cycling. 
3.1.4. Landforms and Tectonic Structures: 
Landforms and tectonic structures encompass features such as mountains, 
valleys, plateaus, volcanoes, and faults.  Their formation is primarily influenced 
by tectonic processes, including earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, and plate 
movements, in conjunction with erosion and weathering over extended periods. 
The Earth's surface is continuously reshaped by these processes. 
3.2. Geological Processes: 
Geological processes shape the Earth's surface through the following processes. 
a. Plate Tectonics : Movement of Earth's crustal plates leads to earthquakes, 
volcanic eruptions, and mountain building. 
b. Weathering and Erosion : Rocks are broken down and transported by wind, 
water, or ice. 
c. Sedimentation: Sediments accumulate in layers and, over time, harden into 
sedimentary rock. 
3.3. Importance of Geosphere: 
Supports ecosystems : The geosphere provides soil for plants to grow and 
shapes habitats for animals and other organisms. 
Source of natural resources : It contains valuable minerals, metals, and fossil 
fuels used for energy, construction, and manufacturing. 
Affects climate: Events like volcanic eruptions release gases and ash that can 
cool or warm the atmosphere. 
Determines physical geography : The geosphere shapes landforms (like 
mountains and valleys), influencing elevation, terrain, and human settlement 
patterns.

3.3. Interaction of the Geosphere with other Earth Spheres: 
The geosphere refers to the solid part of the Earth, including rocks, minerals, 
landforms, and the processes that shape them. It interacts continuously with the 
other Earth spheres — atmosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, and cryosphere — 
creating a dynamic Earth system. 
Geosphere and Atmosphere : Volcanic eruptions release gases like carbon 
dioxide and ash into the atmosphere, affecting air quality and climate. 
Conversely, atmospheric weathering (like wind and rain) breaks down rocks in 
the geosphere. 
Geosphere and Hydrosphere: Rivers erode landforms and transport sediments, 
shaping valleys and coastlines. Groundwater moves through soil and rocks, 
influencing erosion and mineral distribution. 
Geosphere and Biosphere : Plants and animals influence soil formation  and 
stability. For example, plant roots break rocks and contribute organic matter to 
soil, while burrowing animals affect soil structure.

4. Biosphere: 
The biosphere is the global sum of all ecosystems where life exists on Earth. It 
encompasses all living organisms and the environ ments with which they 
interact, ranging from microscopic bacteria in the soil to the largest whales in 
the oceans. The biosphere includes land, water, and atmospheric layers that 
support life. 
 
4.1. Major Components of the Biosphere: 
4.1.1. Living Organisms 
a.Plants: Function as primary producers by converting sunlight into energy 
through photosynthesis. They form the base of food chains. 
b.Animals: Rely on plants (herbivores) or on other animals 
(carnivores/omnivores) for food, acting as consumers.

c. Microorganisms: Bacteria, fungi, algae, and other microbes decompose dead 
organic matter, recycle nutrients, and contribute to soil fertility. 
 
4.2. Ecosystems 
An ecosystem is a community of living organisms (biotic factors) interacting 
with non -living components (abiotic factors) such as sunlight, soil, air, and 
water. Examples of ecosystems found within the biosphere include: 
 Forests 
 Grasslands 
 Deserts 
 Freshwater ecosystems (rivers, lakes) 
 Marine ecosystems (oceans, coral reefs) 
Each ecosystem supports different forms and levels of biodiversity. 
4.3. Functions and Importance of the Biosphere: 
Supports Life : Provides essentials like food, water, oxygen, and habitats for 
organisms. 
Maintains Biodiversity : Supports a wide variety of species, increasing 
ecosystem resilience to changes and disruptions. 
Regulates Biogeochemical Cycles:

The biosphere, which includes all living organisms on Earth , plants, animals, 
fungi, and microorganisms actively regulates biogeochemical cycles by driving 
the flow and transformation of essential elements like carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, 
and phosphorus. 
 
Carbon Cycle : Plants in the biosphere absorb carbon dioxide from the 
atmosphere during photosynthesis, storing carbon in their tissues. Animals 
consume these plants, and carbon moves through the food chain. When 
organisms breathe, decompose, or burn (in the case of fossil fuels), carbon is 
released back into the atmosphere or soil. 
Nitrogen Cycle: Certain bacteria in the biosphere (especially in soil and plant 
roots) convert atmospheric nitrogen into forms that plants can use. Plants absorb 
this nitrogen, and it moves through the food chain. Other microbes return 
nitrogen to the atmosphere through processes like denitrification. 
Oxygen Cycle: Plants produce oxygen through p hotosynthesis, which animals 
use for respiration. In turn, animals release carbon dioxide, which plants use 
again, maintaining a balanced oxygen-carbon relationship. 
Phosphorus Cycle : Living organisms help move phosphorus by absorbing it 
from the soil, usi ng it in DNA and energy transfer. When plants and animals 
die, decomposers break them down, returning phosphorus to the soil for reuse.
