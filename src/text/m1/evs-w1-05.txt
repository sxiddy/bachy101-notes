ENERGY FLOW IN AN ECOSYSTEM 
1. Definition 
An ecosystem is a structural and functional unit of ecology consisting of living organisms 
(biotic components) interacting with each other and with their physical environment 
(abiotic components), with a flow of energy and cycling of matter between them. 
1.1 Biotic Components 
All living organisms in an ecosystem are classified by their nutritional relationship: 
i. Producers (Autotrophs): Organisms that manufacture their own food using an external 
energy source. Primary producers use sunlight (photoautotrophs) — examples: grasses, 
trees, algae, phytoplankton, cyanobacteria. Some use chemical energy 
(chemoautotrophs) — examples: deep-sea hydrothermal vent bacteria. 
ii. Consumers (Heterotrophs): Organisms that cannot make their own food and must 
consume others. 
 Primary Consumers (Herbivores): Feed directly on producers. Examples: deer, 
rabbit, grasshopper, zooplankton, cattle. 
 Secondary Consumers (Carnivores): Feed on herbivores. Examples: frogs, foxes, 
small fish, spiders. 
 Tertiary Consumers (Top Carnivores): Feed on secondary consumers. Examples: 
eagles, tigers, sharks, orcas. 
 Omnivores: Feed on both plants and animals. Examples: humans, bears, crows, pigs. 
iii. Decomposers (Saprotrophs): Break down dead organic matter into inorganic nutrients. 
Examples: bacteria (Bacillus, Pseudomonas) and fungi (Aspergillus, Rhizopus). 
Essential for nutrient recycling. 
iv. Detritivores: Ingest fragments of dead o rganic matter. Examples: earthworms, 
millipedes, woodlice, dung beetles. Differ from decomposers which secrete 
extracellular enzymes.

1.2 Abiotic Components 
i. Solar radiation: Primary energy input. ~1.7 × 10¹⁷ W received by Earth; only ~0.023% 
captured by photosynthesis. 
ii. Temperature: Controls metabolic rates (Q10 rule: rate doubles per 10°C rise). 
iii. Water: Universal solvent; required for photosynthesis and all biochemical reactions. 
iv. Soil: Provides anchorage, water, and nutrients (N, P, K, micronutrients). 
v. Atmospheric gases: O₂ for respiration; CO₂ for photosynthesis; N₂ for nitrogen fixation. 
vi. pH, wind, topography: Influence organism distribution and species composition.

2. Producers & Photosynthesis- The Energy Gateway 
Key Principle: All energy in an ecosystem originates from the Sun and enters through 
PRODUCERS via photosynthesis. Without producers, no other life can exist. 
 
2.1 The Photosynthesis Reaction 
Photosynthesis is the process by which producers convert light energy into chemical energy 
stored in glucose: 

=== page 3 ===
3 
 
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Light Energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ 
i. CO₂ is absorbed from the atmosphere through stomata in leaves. 
ii. H₂O is absorbed from soil through roots via xylem. 
iii. Light energy (photons) is absorbed by chlorophyll pigments in chloroplasts. 
iv. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) stores the captured energy as chemical bond energy. 
v. O₂ is released as a by-product — essential for aerobic respiration of all consumers.

NPP is the actual energy available to consumers and determines the carrying capacity of 
the ecosystem. Tropical rainforests have the highest NPP (~2,000 g/m²/yr); deserts have 
the lowest (~100 g/m²/yr). 
 
2.3 Chemosynthesis — Energy Without Sunlight 
In deep-sea ecosystems (hydrothermal vents, cold seeps), producers use chemical energy 
instead of light energy. Chemoautotrophs oxidize inorganic compounds (H₂S, NH₄, Fe²⁺) 
to fix CO₂. These unique ecosystems support life independ ently of solar energy, 
demonstrating that photosynthesis is not the only route for energy entry into ecosystems.

3. Food Chains & Food Webs 
3.1 Food Chain — Linear Energy Pathway 
A food chain is a simple, linear sequence showing the feeding relationships and energy 
transfer between organisms in an ecosystem. 
 
A. Grazing Food Chain (starts from living green plants) 
Sun → Grass (T1) → Grasshopper (T2) → Frog (T3) → Snake (T4) → Eagle (T5) 
Characteristics: 
i. Begins with living producers (green plants, algae). 
ii. Most common type in terrestrial and marine ecosystems. 
iii. Energy flows from lower to higher trophic levels. 
iv. Maximum 4–5 links due to 10% energy transfer rule. 
 
B. Detritus Food Chain (starts from dead organic matter) 
Dead organic matter → Detritivores (earthw orms, millipedes) → Decomposers 
(fungi, bacteria) → Inorganic nutrients → Soil → Producers 
Characteristics: 
i. Begins with dead plant/animal material (detritus). 
ii. Dominant pathway in forest and wetland ecosystems (80–90% of energy flows through 
here). 
iii. Powered by decomposers — releases nutrients back to soil. 
iv. Does NOT require sunlight directly — operates in dark soil layers. 
 
3.2 Food Web — Complex Reality 
A food web is an interconnected network of multiple food chains in an ecosystem, showing 
all possible feeding relationships simultaneously. 

=== page 5 ===
5 
 
i. More realistic than a food chain — most organisms eat multiple prey species. 
ii. Provides ecosystem stability: if one prey species declines, predators can shift to 
alternatives. 
iii. Redundancy in pathways: energy can flow through multiple routes. 
iv. Loss of a keystone species (e.g., sea otter, wolf) can collapse the entire web. 
 
Classic Example — Yellowstone Trophic Cascade: When wolves were reintroduced in 
1995, they preyed on deer → deer moved away from riverbanks → vegetation recovered 
→ rivers changed course (less erosion). This cascade effect through the food web altered 
the entire ecosystem, including its physical geography.

5. Laws of Thermodynamics & Energy Transfer 
5.1 First Law of Thermodynamics: Conservation of Energy 
"Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transformed from one form 
to another." 
Application to Ecosystems: 
i. Solar energy (radiant) → Chemical energy (glucose via photosynthesis) 
ii. Chemical energy (food) → Kinetic energy (movement) + Thermal energy (body heat) 
+ Chemical energy (growth) 
iii. The TOTAL energy at each step is conserved — none is created or destroyed, only 
transformed. 
 
5.2 Second Law of Thermodynamics — Entropy 
"In any energy transformation, some energy is inevitably lost as heat, increasing the 
total entropy of the universe." 
 

=== page 8 ===
8 
 
Application to Ecosystems: 
i. Every energy transformati on (photosynthesis, eating, respiration) produces heat that 
cannot be recovered. 
ii. This is WHY energy flows in only ONE direction (sun → producers → consumers) and 
cannot be recycled. 
iii. This is WHY matter MUST be recycled through biogeochemical cycles — but energy 
cannot be. 
iv. This is WHY ecosystems require a constant, continuous input of solar energy to 
function.

level is transferred to the next trophic level. The remaining 90% is lost.

6. Biogeochemical Cycles: Matter Recycling 
 
Key Principle: Unlike energy (which flows in one direction and is lost), MATTER is 
continuously recycled between organisms and the environment through biogeochemical 
cycles. These cycles are driven partly by energy flow and partly by geological and 
biological processes. 
 
6.1 The Carbon Cycle 
Carbon i s the backbone of all organic molecules. It cycles between the atmosphere, 
organisms, oceans, and lithosphere. 
 
Step 1 — Fixation: Plants absorb CO₂ from the atmosphere during photosynthesis, 
converting it to organic carbon (glucose, cellulose, starch). 
Step 2 — Consumption: Carbon passes through the food chain as organisms consume 
one another. Organic compounds are ingested and assimilated. 
Step 3 — Respiration: All organisms (producers and consumers) release CO₂ back to 
the atmosphere through cellular respiration: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ATP. 
Step 4 — Decomposition: Decomposers (bacteria, fungi) break down dead organisms 
and organic waste, releasing CO₂ and inorganic nutrients to the soil and atmosphere. 
Step 5 — Combustion: Burning of fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and forests releases 
geologically stored carbon as CO₂, enhancing the greenhouse effect. 
Step 6 — Ocean absorption: Oceans dissolve atmospheric CO₂, acting as a major carbon 
sink (~30% of anthropogenic CO₂ absorbed).

Human Impact on Carbon Cycle: Pre-industrial atmospheric CO₂ was ~280 ppm. Current 
levels (2024) exceed 422 ppm — highest in 3 million years. Burning fossil fuels releases 
36 billion tonnes of CO₂ annually, far exceeding natural recycling capacity.

6.3 The Phosphorus Cycle 
Phosphorus has no atmospheric phase — it cycles between rocks, soil, water, and 
organisms. It is often the limiting nutrient in freshwater ecosystems. 
i. Weathering of phosphate rocks releases H₂PO₄⁻ into soil. 
ii. Plants absorb phosphate → incorporated into ATP, DNA, RNA, cell membranes 
(phospholipids). 
iii. Consumers obtain P by eating producers. Decomposers return P to soil. 
iv. Excess P from agricultural runoff causes algal blooms in lakes (eutrophication). 
 
7. Real-World Applications of Energy Flow  
7.1 Dietary Efficiency & Vegetarianism 
The 10% law has profound implications for food production: 
i. Eating at T1 (plants) directly: 10,000 kJ of plant energy → 10,000 kJ available to 
human. 
ii. Eating at T2 (herbivores/livestock): 10,000 kJ of plant energy → 1,000 kJ available via 
animal → ~100 kJ to human. 
iii. Producing 1 kg of beef requires ~8 kg of grain, 15,000 litres of water, and 20× more 
land than 1 kg of plant food. 
iv. Shifting diets toward plant-based foods is one of the most powerful individual actions 
to reduce environmental impact.

7.3 Conservation of Top Predators 
Energy flow understanding explains why top predators are critical ecosystem regulators 
despite their small numbers: 
i. Wolves in Yellowstone: Reintroduction (1995) reduced deer grazing → vegetation 
recovered → riverbank stabilized → biodiversity increased. 
ii. Sharks in marine ecosystems: Remove sick/weak fish → maintain fish population 
health → support reef ecosystems. 
iii. Loss of top predators causes trophic cascade — changes at all levels propagate 
downward.

7.4 Sustainable Agriculture & Aquaculture 
i. Polyculture farming (multiple crops together) mimics natural food webs — more 
efficient and resilient. 
ii. Harvesting lower trophic levels (sardines, anchovies, mussels) is more sustainable than 
top predators. 
iii. Integrated farming (fish + rice paddies) uses waste from one species as resource for 
another — mimics ecosystem cycling. 
 
8. Summary & Key Takeaways 
 
"Energy flows through ecosystems in one direction — from the Sun to producers to 
consumers — decreasing at each step. Matter, however, cycles continuously and is never 
lost." 
 
i. An ecosystem consists of biotic (producers, consumers, decomposers) and abiotic 
(light, water, temperature, soil) components interacting through energy flow and matter 
cycling. 
ii. Producers are the only entry point for energy into any ecosystem — via photosynthesis 
(or chemosynthesis). NPP = GPP − Respiration. 
iii. Food chains (linear) and food webs (complex networks) describe energy transfer 
pathways. Detritus chains handle 80–90% of ecosystem energy in forests. 
iv. Ecological pyramids (numbers, biomass, energy) represent trophic structure. Pyramid 
of energy is ALWAYS upright. 
v. Lindemann's 10% Law: Only ~10% of energy at one trophic level is transferred to the 
next. The rest (90%) is lost as heat, respiration, and undigested matter. 
vi. 1st Law: Energy is conserved (transformed). 2nd Law: Energy degrades to heat — this 
drives the one-directional flow and limits food chain length. 
vii. Carbon cycle (photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition) and Nitrogen cycle (fixation, 
nitrification, denitrification) recycle matter continuously. 
viii. Applications: Shorter food chains = more efficient. Biomagnification concentrates 
toxins. Keystone predators regulate trophic cascades.
