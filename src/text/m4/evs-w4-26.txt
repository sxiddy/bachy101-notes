1. Introduction 
Climate change is caused by greenhouse gas emissions from human activity, primarily 
the burning of fossil fuels and deforestation. The atmosphere is a global challenge: 
emissions released anywhere affect the climate everywhere, and no single country can 
solve this problem alone. That fundamental reality is why international law is necessary, 
and why the Paris Agreement exists. 
Think of the atmosphere as a shared resource that belongs to all of humanity. When one 
country pollutes it, every country suffers the consequences. That is the reason nearly 
two hundred nations sat down together in Paris in 2015 and negotiated a common plan. 
The Paris Agreement represents a global commitment by nations to work together to 
combat climate change and create a more sustainable future.

3. The Historical Road to Paris 
To understand the Paris Agreement, it helps to know the thirty -year backstory of 
international climate negotiations that led up to it. Three milestones are especially 
important. 

=== page 3 ===
3.1  The UNFCCC, 1992 — The Foundation 
The United Nations Framework Convention on  Climate Change (UNFCCC) was born 
at the Rio Earth Summit in 1992. It set the ultimate objective of stabilising greenhouse 
gas concentrations in the atmosphere to prevent dangerous interference with the climate 
system. It also introduced the principle of Common But Differentiated Responsibilities: 
all nations share the obligation to act, but industrialised countries, which caused most 
historic emissions, must lead. The UNFCCC is the parent treaty, and the Paris 
Agreement is one of its children. 
3.2  The Kyoto Protocol, 1997 — The First Binding Targets 
The Kyoto Protocol was the first legally binding agreement with specific emission 
reduction targets, but only for developed countries. The United States signed it but never 
ratified it, and Canada later withdre w. Major emerging economies such as China and 
India had no binding commitments under it. Global emissions continued to rise, and it 
became clear that a new, more inclusive framework was needed. 
3.3  Copenhagen to Paris, 2009-2015 — Building the New Architecture 
The 2009 Copenhagen conference failed to produce a binding treaty, but it introduced 
the idea of voluntary national pledges, which later evolved into NDCs. Six years of 
subsequent negotiations built the trust and consensus that eventually made Paris 
possible. At COP21 in Paris in December 2015, 196 parties adopted the Agreement to 
a standing ovation, and it entered into force on 4 November 2016. From a diplomatic 
standpoint, Paris was remarkable: getting nearly two hundred countries, with vastly 
different economies, histories, and interests, to agree on a common climate framework 
was an achievement many thought impossible after the Copenhagen failure. 
4. The Temperature Goal and Nationally Determined Contributions 
The Paris Agreement has several interconnected elements. The two most central are the 
temperature goal and the NDC system. 
4.1  The Temperature Goal — Article 2 
Article 2 outlines the treaty's central objective: to hold the increase in global average 
temperature to well below 2 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels, while pursuing 
efforts to limit the increase to 1.5 degrees Celsius. This more ambitious 1.5-degree target 
was included at the insistence of Small Island Developing States (SIDS), which face 
existential threats from sea-level rise. 

=== page 4 ===
Why does half a degree matter? The IPCC's 2018 Special Report gave the answer. At 
1.5 degrees Celsius, coral reefs face severe stress; at 2 degrees Celsius, they are almost 
entirely lost. At 1.5 degrees Celsius, hundreds of millions fewer people face  climate-
related water scarcity compared with 2 degrees Celsius. Half a degree is the difference 
between a difficult future and a catastrophic one for hundreds of millions of people. 
4.2  Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) — The Engine of the 
Agreement 
Every country sets its own climate targets, action plans, and emission reduction 
strategies — these together form its Nationally Determined Contribution (NDC). Unlike 
Kyoto's top-down assigned targets, Paris uses a bottom-up approach in which countries 
determine their own level of ambition. 
NDCs must be submitted  every five years, and each new submission must be more 
ambitious than the last. This is called the ratchet mechanism — a mechanism that moves 
in one direction and cannot go back. NDCs typically cover three areas: mitigation targets 
(cutting emissions), adaptation plans (coping with climate impacts), and finance needs. 
In short, the Paris Agreement does not tell a country what its target must be; it says that 
the country decides its own target, but every five years it must return with a higher one. 
The direction is prescribed; the pace is national. 
4.3  India's NDC 
India's Nationally Determined Contribution includes several concrete, measurable 
commitments: 
• 50 percent of installed power capacity from non-fossil fuel sources by 2030. 
• 45 percent reduction in the emissions intensity of GDP from 2005 levels by 2030. 
• An additional carbon sink of 2.5 to 3 billion tonnes of CO2-equivalent through 
forest and tree cover. 
5. Net-Zero, Adaptation, and Finance 
Beyond the temperature goal and NDCs, the Paris Agreement res ts on three further 
pillars: the net-zero goal, adaptation, and finance. 
5.1  Net-Zero Emissions — Article 4 
Article 4 sets a long-term goal: to reach global peaking of emissions as soon as possible, 
and then rapidly reduce emissions to net -zero in the sec ond half of this century. Net -

zero means that the emissions entering the atmosphere are equal to the emissions being 
removed by forests, oceans, or technology. This implies a complete transformation of 
energy, transport, industry, and buildings — all of them engineering domains. 
Net-zero does not mean zero emissions; it means balance. Think of it like a bank 
account: you can spend, but you must deposit an equal amount. The world must reach a 
point where every tonne of CO2 emitted is offset by a tonne removed. 
5.2  Adaptation — Article 7 
Adaptation is placed on equal footing with mitigation in the Paris Agreement — it is 
not an afterthought. The global goal is to enhance adaptive capacity, strengthen 
resilience, and reduce vulnerability to climate impacts. Countries must prepare 
adaptation plans and report on  their adaptation actions and needs. For India, this 
includes flood-resistant infrastructure, drought-tolerant agriculture, coastal protection, 
and urban heat management — all of which are engineering tasks. 
5.3  Climate Finance — Article 9 
Developed count ries committed to mobilise USD 100 billion per year to support 
developing nations' climate action, with the Green Climate Fund serving as the primary 
channel for this finance. This USD 100 billion target was repeatedly missed and is 
widely considered inade quate, since the actual need runs into trillions of dollars 
annually. A new, higher finance goal was to be agreed before 2025, and this remains a 
live and contested negotiation. 
Finance is where international climate negotiations most often break down. Developing 
countries cannot afford a low-carbon transition using their own resources alone — they 
need support. Whether developed nations deliver on their finance commitments remains 
one of the most contested questions in climate diplomacy today, and India ha s 
consistently taken a strong position on equity and climate finance in these debates. 
6. Transparency, the Global Stocktake, and Legal Character 
Two further features of the Agreement are worth understanding: how it checks whether 
countries are delivering on their promises, and the question of whether it is actually 
legally binding. 
6.1  Transparency Framework and Global Stocktake 

=== page 6 ===
The Enhanced Transparency Framework requires all parties to regularly report their 
greenhouse gas emissions, their progress on NDCs, and their climate finance flows. The 
Global Stocktake is a collective review conducted every five years that assesses whether 
the world is on track to meet the Agreement's goals. The first Global Stocktake was 
completed at COP28 in Dubai in 2023, and its conclusion was that while progress has 
been made, current NDCs are far insufficient to meet the 1.5 -degree goal, and 
accelerated action is urgently needed. 
The Global Stocktake functions as the Agreement's reality check. Every five years, the 
world holds up a mirror to itself and asks whether it is doing enough. In 2023, the answer 
was clearly no, and that finding is now expected to push countries to set more ambitious 
NDCs in the next round. 
6.2  Legal Character of the Agreement 
The legal character of the Paris Agreement requires a nuanced answer. The Agreement 
itself is legally binding as a treaty — it is registered with the United Nations and entered 
into force in November 2016. However, individual NDC targets are not legally binding 
under international law, since countries determine their own targets. What is legally 
binding is the obligation to submit NDCs, report transparently, and raise ambition over 
time; no enforcement mechanism exists to penalise a country for missing its stated 
targets. The United States' withdrawal from the Agreement in 2020 and its re -entry in 
2021 illustrated the Agreement's vulnerability to domestic politics. 
Critics call this arrangement toothless, while proponents argue that transparency and 
reputational pressure act as th e real enforcement mechanism, since no government 
wants to be named as a climate laggard on the world stage. The honest answer is that it 
is a legally binding process with nationally determined substance — whether that is 
enough remains one of the central debates in international environmental law. 
7. Challenges and Engineering Relevance 
7.1  The Ambition Gap 
If all current NDCs were fully implemented, the world would still be heading for 2.5 to 
3 degrees Celsius of warming — far above the 1.5-degree target. The gap between what 
countries have promised and what the science requires is the central challenge of climate 
governance today. 
7.2  The Finance Gap 

=== page 7 ===
The USD 100 billion per year target was only, and controversially, declared met for the 
year 2022 — several years late. Actual needs for developing nations run into trillions of 
dollars, and the gap between promise and delivery continues to erode trust between 
developed and developing nations. 
7.3  Loss and Damage 
A fund to compensate nations for irreversibl e climate harms was established formally 
at COP27 in 2022 and operationalised at COP28. Funding levels for this Loss and 
Damage Fund remain far below what vulnerable nations need, and climate justice 
remains an unresolved issue in international negotiations. 
7.4  Engineering Relevance 
Every sector targeted for decarbonisation in NDCs is an engineering sector: energy, 
buildings, transport, industry, and agriculture. Renewable energy scale -up, net -zero 
buildings, electric transport, and low -carbon industrial processes all require engineers 
to design, build, and operate them. Major infrastructure projects must now demonstrate 
climate risk analysis and NDC alignment to access international finance. 
Understanding the Paris Agreement is not optional for engineers working in these 
sectors — it defines the regulatory and financial landscape of their careers. The Paris 
Agreement is not just a diplomat's document; it is a blueprint for rebuilding how the 
world produces energy, moves people, constructs buildings, and runs industries. Every 
one of those tasks is an engineering task, and today's engineering students are training 
at exactly the moment when this transformation is accelerating. 
8. Summary 
Climate change is a global challenge, but solutions begin with informed  minds and 
responsible actions. The Paris Agreement reminds us that sustainability is a shared 
responsibility, not a choice. Its success depends not only on governments but also on 
the next generation of engineers, scientists, and leaders — the ideas of today's students 
can become the climate solutions of tomorrow.

Quick Recap — Five Things to Remember 
– The Paris Agreement (2015) is the world's primary international legal 
framework for climate action, adopted under the UNFCCC by 196 parties. 
– Its core goal is to limit global warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius, with 
efforts towards 1.5 degrees Celsius above pre-industrial levels. 
– Each country sets its own NDC — a climate action plan that must be updated 
every five years with progressively higher ambition. 
– The Agreement is legally binding as a treaty, but individual NDC targets are 
nationally determined, not internationally prescribed. 
– For engineers, it defines the transformation of energy, buildings, transport, and 
industry that their professional careers will be spent delivering.
