2. Introduction 
The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 was the first c omprehensive 
water pollution law in independent India. Enacted by Parliament on March 23, 1974, its 
primary objective is preventing and controlling water pollution while maintaining or 
restoring the wholesomeness of water. It also created the institutional architecture that still 
governs environmental regulation across the country today. 
Why was It Needed? 
India's major rivers — the Ganga, Yamuna, Godavari, and Cauvery — are ecological, 
cultural, and economic lifelines for hundreds of millions of people. Decades of industrial 
discharge, untreated sewage, and agricultural runoff had severely degraded many of these 
systems, creating the pressure for national legislation.

Constitutional Basis 
The Act was enacted under Article 252 of the Constitution, which allo ws Parliament to 
legislate on subjects normally reserved for states when state legislatures pass resolutions 
requesting it. Several states requested a central law on water pollution, which is how the 
Act achieved national application despite water being a state subject. 
The 1988 amendment significantly strengthened the Act — penalties were enhanced and 
Board powers were expanded, improving enforcement effectiveness. 
Institutional Significance 
The Water Act, 1974 is the legislation that established the Central Pollution Control Board 
(CPCB) and the State Pollution Control Boards (SPCBs). These same bodies later took on 
responsibilities under the Air Act as well — so studying the Water Act means studying the 
origin point of India's entire environmental regulatory infrastructure. 
• CPCB: sets national water quality standards and provides technical guidance. 
• SPCB: grants consent, monitors rivers and wells, operates testing laboratories, and 
prosecutes polluters.

3. Key Provisions 
The Act contains 64 sections divided into 10 chapters. The provisions below are the ones 
engineering students must understand. 
3.1 Institutional Framework — Pollution Control Boards 
Central Pollution Control Board (CPCB) — Section 3 
Established at the national level. Main functions include: 
• Promoting cleanliness of streams and wells in different areas 
• Advising the Central Government on water pollution prevention and control 
• Coordinating activities of State Boards 
• Providing technical assistance and training 

=== page 3 ===
• Conducting and sponsoring research on water pollution 
• Collecting and disseminating information on water pollution 
State Pollution Control Boards (SPCB) — Section 4 
Each state must establish a State Board responsible for: 
• Advising the State Government on water pollution matters 
• Planning and executing comprehensive programs for pollution prevention 
• Inspecting sewage and trade effluent treatment plants 
• Reviewing plans for wastewater treatment facilities 
• Collecting and disseminating water quality information 
• Training personnel for pollution control programs 
Joint Boards — Section 13 
Two or more states may form a Joint Board to handle pollution control jointly — useful 
for rivers flowing through multiple states. 
3.2 Prohibition on Discharge of Polluting Matter 
Section 24 — Prohibition on Use of Stream or Well for Disposal of Polluting Matter 
An absolute prohibition: no person may knowingly allow poisonous, noxious, or polluting 
matter to enter any stream or well. Applies to factories, municipalities, farms, and every 
other entity — there are no e xceptions except discharges permitted under State Board 
consent. 
• Discharging any poisonous, noxious, or polluting matter into a stream or well is 
prohibited 
• Discharging matter that would deteriorate water quality is prohibited 
• Applies to both liquid and solid pollutants 
• Exceptions exist only for discharges permitted under State Board consent 
Engineering relevance: any industrial process generating wastewater must treat it before 
discharge, and discharge may only occur with proper consent. 
Section 25 — Restrictions on New Outlets and New Discharges  
Establishes the consent mechanism for water — mirroring what Section 21 does for air. 
Any industry likely to discharge trade effluent must obtain prior consent from the State 
Board before beginning operations. The Board may specify conditions, such as requiring a 
particular wastewater treatment plant to be in place before discharge begins. 
• Prior consent from the State Board is required before creating a new discharge outlet 
• New discharges of sewage or trade effluent are prohibited without consent 
• The consent application must detail the nature and quantity of discharge 

=== page 4 ===
• The State Board can approve, reject, or impose conditions on consent 
Engineering relevance: engineers designing new industrial facilities must seek consent 
before establishing any discharge outlet. 
Section 26 — Provision Regarding Existing Discharge of Sewage or Trade Effluent  
• Persons already discharging sewage or effluent must apply for consent within a 
specified time period 
• Existing discharges without consent are prohibited after the deadline 
• The State Board can grant consent with conditions, or refuse it 
Ensures that even older industries must comply with pollution control requirements. 
3.3 Powers of Inspection and Sample Collection  
Section 21 — Power to Take Samples of Effluents  
State Board officers can: 
• Take samples of effluents from any outlet or discharge point 
• Follow specific sampling procedures, including notifying the person concerned 
• Send samples to recognized laboratories for analysis 
• Use analysis results as evidence in legal proceedings 
Section 23 — Power of Entry and Inspection 
Board officers have the authority to: 
• Enter any place for inspection purposes 
• Inspect sewage or trade effluent treatment plants 
• Examine records and documents 
• Check compliance with consent conditions 
3.4 Consent Mechanism and Effluent Standards 
• Industries must obtain consent from the State Board before discharging effluents 
• Consent is granted with specific conditions on effluent quality, quantity, and 
monitoring 
• The State Board can specify effluent standards based on the receiving water body's 
capacity 
• Consent can be refused if the discharge would cause unacceptable pollution 
• Consent can be withdrawn if conditions are violated 
3.5 Functions and Powers of Boards  
• Section 16 — Functions of Central Board: advisory, coordinating, and research 
functions at the national level 

=== page 5 ===
• Section 17 — Functions of State Board: inspection, monitoring, and enforcement 
within the state 
• Section 18 — Power to Give Directions: both Boards  can give legally binding 
directions to governments, industries, and any person regarding compliance 
3.6 Emergency Measures  
• Section 32 — Emergency Measures: State Boards can remove pollutants causing 
imminent danger, restore water quality after accidents, and prevent further pollution 
during emergencies 
• Section 33 — Power to Apply to Courts: Boards can seek court orders to stop 
apprehended pollution, prevent discharge of pollutants, or restrict polluting activities 
3.7 Appeals and Revision 
• Section 28 — Appeals: persons aggrieved by State Board decisions (e.g. refusal of 
consent) can appeal to the State Government 
• Section 29 — Revision: the State Government can revise any order passed by the State 
Board 
• Section 33B — Appeal to National Green Tribunal (NGT): a fter the NGT's 
establishment, Water Act appeals can be filed with it directly

Important Points About Penalties 
1. Non-compoundable offenses: most offenses under th e Water Act are non -
compoundable — they cannot be settled by paying a fine alone; court proceedings are 
mandatory. 
2. Cognizable offenses: some offenses are cognizable, allowing police to arrest without 
a warrant. 
3. Environmental Protection Fund: penalty amounts are credited to the Environmental 
Protection Fund for restoration projects. 
4. Continuing liability: even after conviction, offenders remain liable for cleanup and 
restoration costs. 
5. Why Engineering Students Must Know the Water Act 
1. Professional Responsibility and Ethical Practice 
As future engineers, you will design systems, manage industrial processes, and make 
decisions that directly impact water quality. Understanding the Act enables you to: 
• Design wastewater treatment systems that meet legal standards 
• Recommend processes that minimize pollution 
• Ensure projects comply with environmental regulations 
• Make ethical decisions that prioritize environmental protection over cost-cutting 
2. Legal Compliance in Industrial Practice 
Nearly all engineering industries — textiles, chemicals, pharmaceuticals, food processing, 
power plants, steel mills — generate wastewater. Compliance is mandatory: 
• Obtain consent before discharging effluents (Section 25) 
• Treat wastewater to meet effluent standards 
• Maintain records and furnish information to the State Board (Section 20) 

=== page 7 ===
• Allow inspections by Board officers (Section 23) 
Non-compliance results in severe penalties, including imprisonment for engineers in 
charge. 
3. Risk Management and Corporate Liability 
Under Section 46, engineers and managers can be personally liable for pollution offenses 
committed by their companies. Awareness helps you: 
• Identify potential violations before they occur 
• Implement compliance systems in your workplace 
• Protect yourself and your organization from legal liability 
• Document due diligence to demonstrate compliance

7. Conclusion 
The Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act, 1974 is India's foundational law for 
protecting water resour ces from pollution. Enacted in response to severe water 
contamination caused by industrialization and urbanization, it established a comprehensive 
legal framework with Central and State Pollution Control Boards, prohibited uncontrolled 

=== page 9 ===
discharge of polluting matter, mandated consent for all discharges, and provided strong 
penalty provisions for violations. 
For first-year engineering students, understanding this Act is not optional — it is essential 
for your professional future. You will design systems, mana ge processes, and make 
decisions that directly affect water quality. Compliance with the Water Act protects public 
health, preserves ecosystems, ensures sustainable water resources, and avoids severe legal 
consequences including imprisonment and fines. 
Key provisions to remember: Section 24 (prohibition on discharge), Section 25 (restrictions 
on new outlets), Section 26 (existing discharge provisions), and penalty Sections 41–45. 
Penalties are severe — imprisonment from 1.5 to 6 years plus fines for serious violations, 
with additional daily penalties for continuing violations. 
Awareness of the Water Act enables you to practice ethically, manage legal risks, pursue 
environmental career opportunities, design sustainable systems, communicate effectively 
with regulators, and contribute to environmental protection.
